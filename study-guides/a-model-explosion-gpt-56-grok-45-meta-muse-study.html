A Model Explosion: GPT-5.6 Sol, Grok 4.5 and Meta Muse Rewrite the Rules

Study Guide

Overview

In this video, AI Explained (Philip) unpacks a frantic 24-hour stretch of frontier model releases: OpenAI's GPT 5.6 family (Soul, Terror, Luna), Grok 4.5 from xAI, and Meta's Muse Spark 1.1. His central thesis is that the competitive story has shifted away from chasing only the very top leaderboard scores. Instead, the labs are now asking a different question: what if you can get almost as good performance at a fraction of the price? He argues this cost-per-performance dynamic, playing out across OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Meta, and Chinese labs, is the real story, and he walks through roughly a dozen benchmarks and demos to make sense of it.

Key Points

  • Three new OpenAI models: GPT 5.6 Soul (paid plans only), Terror, and Luna, positioned against Anthropic's Fable, Opus, and Sonnet respectively, generally at about a third of the cost.
  • Cost is the headline: OpenAI's models tend to cost roughly a third of the comparable Anthropic "Fable series" while often not sacrificing performance, and sometimes leading.
  • Agent's Last Exam: GPT 5.6 Soul on extra high scored almost 54% versus Fable on max at 45%, on a benchmark co-led by UC Berkeley covering 55 industries with 300 experts.
  • Cheaper rivals complicate OpenAI's story: Grok 4.5 leads on SWE Marathon, and near-frontier performance is available from GLM 5.2, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Meta's Muse Spark at dramatically lower cost.
  • Meta Muse Spark 1.1: scores 72% on Vibe Code Bench versus Soul's 81%, but at around 35 times less cost, reinforcing the cost-efficiency theme for prosumer use cases.
  • Verifiable domains get crushed: Philip argues that once a domain is verifiable, a model will eventually ace it, and sub-100% scores reflect messy data, thin training data, or limited reasoning budget rather than fundamental limits.

Detailed Sections

The Cost Reframe and "I Smell Fear"

The video opens with the observation that we were "only supposed to care about the very top scores" and the best vibes in our own workflows, but three frontier labs just reframed the question around getting almost as good at a fraction of the price. Philip notes that the lead for OpenAI's "Super app" replied to an Anthropic post that was giving away more usage with the words "I smell fear." He also flags, without dwelling on it, a "somewhat disquieting" post and paper from Anthropic about AI consciousness, which he covers in depth on his Patreon.

The Three OpenAI Models and Effort Levels

OpenAI released GPT 5.6 Soul, Terror, and Luna. Soul is only available on paid plans and combines with many effort levels (on first sight it looks like five, plus a hidden promo). Philip says the observations hold broadly across the board: comparing Soul versus Fable, Terror versus Opus, or Luna versus Sonnet, the OpenAI model generally costs about a third of the Anthropic "flawed/Fable series," and this does not always mean worse performance.

Agent's Last Exam and the AI-First Argument

OpenAI highlighted Agent's Last Exam, where GPT 5.6 Soul on extra high scored almost 54% versus Fable going all out on max at 45%. The benchmark was co-led by UC Berkeley, covers 55 industries, and involved 300 experts crafting long-horizon, economically valuable tasks. Lead Dawn Song said "every task is derived from a real project that a human expert previously completed. No vibes, no human judges, fully reproducible." Philip argues we never needed to pass 90% on coding benchmarks for developers to switch to AI-first coding, so finance and other white-collar domains might flip to AI-first by the end of the year. He cites a Bloomberg report that a firm (Corey) is backlogged by tens of billions of dollars in demand from financial firms.

Contamination, AutomationBench, and Coding Benchmarks

Because Agent's Last Exam is new, its answers are harder to find contaminated in GPT 5.6's training data, as is Zapier's AutomationBench, which tests agents on end-to-end workflow execution across sales, marketing, operations, support, finance, and HR. On AutomationBench the lead is less stark: a 0.7% score lead for Soul on max at nearly the same cost as Fable. On a GDP-value benchmark Fable actually has a slightly higher Elo but at triple the cost. Artificial Analysis combined multiple coding benchmarks into one aggregate index, where GPT 5.6 Soul scored highest at 80 versus Fable's 77, at lower cost, seemingly reinforced by Terminal Bench 2.1 (though that overlaps with the same index and DeepSWE).

Where Cheaper Models Lead: SWE Marathon and Grok

Philip cautions that harder benchmarks Frontier SWE and SWE Marathon did not have GPT Soul results published. SWE Marathon involves multi-hour tasks with tens of millions of tokens per trial, and there Grok 4.5 leads, with Fable 5 trailing. He credits the data Grok now has from the Cursor acquisition by xAI as helping propel it. He notes the same cheaper-but-almost-as-good logic that tempts a move from Fable 5 to GPT 5.6 Soul might instead nudge users toward Grok 4.5 or the still-cheaper Chinese model GLM 5.2, which makes OpenAI's curves look less appealing.

ARC-AGI 3, Competitive Coding, and Playable Games

On the abstract pattern recognition benchmark ARC-AGI 3, graded to be especially penalizing, GPT 5.6 Soul still does well, getting 8% versus other models struggling below 2% (Anthropic reportedly did not even run Fable due to cost). In the last 24 hours an OpenAI model, possibly internal, broke a competitive coding benchmark, which Philip treats as a warning shot that verifiable domains eventually get crushed. He notes a new class of benchmarks where companies ship entire playable games in release notes to show off careful, ergonomic, functional interfaces and browser use. Running the same prompt he used for Fable on GPT 5.6 Soul Ultra produced a Pokemon-clone game set in the Redwall universe, with a better title page than Fable's, though 5.6 twice messed up the sound settings. He got results in 20 minutes where Fable took more than an hour.

Meta Muse Spark and Cheap Near-Frontier Models

Meta's Muse Spark 1.1 is celebrated for vibe coding; on Vibe Code Bench (from independent lab Vowels AI) it scored 72% versus Soul's 81%, but at around 35 times less cost. Philip notes that for game design, website mockups, and consumer or prosumer use cases, you may not need Soul or even the lighter Luna (still four times more expensive and much slower on that benchmark). He flags a data-contamination caveat: Muse Spark's high score on Humanity's Last Exam is suspect because that exam was co-created by Scale AI, which Meta bought.

SimpleBench, Ultra Mode, and Self-Improvement

On his own private benchmark, SimpleBench (a trick-question / common-sense reasoning benchmark over two years old, recently cited in The Economist and by MIT Tech Review), OpenRouter exposes an unannounced "pro" version of the models. Soul Pro scored 71.7%, still 10% lower than Fable and not far ahead of Grok 4.5, while Soul itself got around 65%. Standouts on his performance-per-dollar frontier chart included Qwen 3.7, GLM 5.2, and DeepSeek V4 Flash (an incredibly cheap model still getting almost 50%). OpenAI's release video demoed a new Ultra mode (akin to Gemini's Deep Think or Claude's Ultra mode) using more parallel agents to finish faster. On self-improvement, OpenAI claimed Soul post-trained Luna, but Philip says these claims are very hard to judge given unknown human review and hand-holding, and he argues we are nowhere close to saturation on post-training benchmarks like PostTrainBench.

Takeaways

  • The frontier race is now defined by cost-per-performance, not just top-line scores; OpenAI's GPT 5.6 models often match rivals at roughly a third of the cost.
  • OpenAI's cheaper-but-nearly-as-good pitch cuts both ways, since Grok 4.5, GLM 5.2, DeepSeek, Qwen, and Meta's Muse Spark offer near-frontier performance for far less.
  • Benchmark newness matters: Agent's Last Exam, AutomationBench, and ARC-AGI 3 are harder to game via training-data contamination, while Scale AI-linked benchmarks raise contamination concerns.
  • Verifiable domains will eventually be aced by models, so many sub-100% scores reflect messy data, thin training data, or limited reasoning budget rather than hard limits.
  • The AI-first transition may spread from coding to finance and other white-collar work by year end, following a review-and-tweak workflow rather than waiting for 90%-plus benchmark scores.
  • Self-improvement claims (Soul post-training Luna) are real in direction but hard to verify, and Philip believes these approaches are far from saturation.
