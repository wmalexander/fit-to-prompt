China's K3 Model Reveals the Problem With Open Weights

Nate B Jones · Nate B Jones · AI News and Strategy Daily · July 20, 2026 · ~19 min

Study Guide

Overview

Nate B Jones uses the release of Kimi K3, a new large open-weight model from the Chinese lab Moonshot, to challenge a comfortable assumption about open source. The common belief is that open-source models are two things: cheap to run and efficient to run. Jones argues that Kimi K3 breaks that belief in important ways, and that understanding why tells us where the AI race is actually heading and where individuals and companies should be putting their personal compute dollars. The open weights are set to drop around July 27, but the strategic lesson is available now.

The throughline: as open models scale toward the frontier, they get bigger and more expensive to serve, not smaller and cheaper. That reality reshapes how Jones thinks about safety, skill, and resilience in the second half of 2026.

Why Kimi K3 Breaks the Open-Weights Assumption

Kimi K3 is a big, heavy model. Jones reports that running it at top performance requires 64 accelerator cores, which he flatly notes almost nobody has at home. That is a corporate installation footprint, not a laptop or hobbyist setup. In exchange for that hardware you get near-frontier performance, especially in coding, approaching (though not matching) Fable 5 coding quality, while falling off in other areas.

Because of the hardware demand, the value proposition is not cheapness or efficiency. And running it in the cloud instead does not rescue the economics: Jones says Kimi's pricing is expensive along two dimensions. First, the per-token price is already in frontier tiers, around 15 dollars per million output tokens, high for a Chinese model. Second, and more subtly, Kimi K3 uses more tokens to reach a given answer than frontier models like OpenAI's 5.5/5.6 or Fable 5. Frontier models may cost more per token but are relatively token-efficient; Kimi K3 is not. So you pay more per token and burn more tokens.

The Efficiency Narrative, Reconsidered

Jones connects this to the old DeepSeek narrative on Wall Street. Training a distilled model requires back propagation, and back propagation itself requires inference. So if a lab were genuinely efficient at the inference inside training, it should also be efficient at serving the model. The fact that Kimi K3 is expensive to serve suggests the story that Chinese labs are uniquely, incredibly efficient may be overstated.

He is careful: this is not a claim that Chinese labs lack innovation. There is real, impressive work happening, and calling it "just distillation" is wrong. But the evidence, he argues, points to OpenAI being very efficient at serving models, Anthropic becoming fairly efficient, and Chinese labs lagging on serving efficiency, which implies the closed-source American labs still hold the knowledge lead.

Benchmarking the Real Frontier

Jones makes a pointed argument about how people measure the gap. Observers compare the released Chinese frontier model to the released American frontier model, but the true American frontier is what sits unreleased inside the labs. Fable, he notes, had been inside the lab for months before the public got it. When you benchmark correctly, Chinese models are still roughly six or seven months behind, the same distance as a year ago. The narrative that they are about to pass 5.6 or Fable by year end is, in his view, simply incorrect, because the labs are already well past what they have publicly shipped. He expects another four to six months before we see what is internal now.

The Scaling Trap for Open Source

A larger implication follows. To push open models toward the frontier, you have to scale them up, there is no free lunch where models magically shrink while matching frontier performance. Bigger models are harder, less efficient, and more expensive to serve, whether you pay in chips or in provider fees. So open source will keep coming with real serving costs and will trail closed source for the foreseeable future. None of this means we should not use open source; Jones calls it a valuable tool in the arsenal and says these models have never been easier to work with. The error is mistaking availability for frontier-level performance.

Key Concepts

  • Open weights vs. open source: Moonshot plans to release Kimi K3's weights (around July 27), letting you run it locally, if you have the hardware.
  • Accelerator-core requirement: 64 accelerator cores for top performance, a corporate-scale footprint.
  • Two-dimensional cost: high per-token pricing plus token-inefficiency (more tokens per answer).
  • Serving efficiency as a signal: inference is embedded in training, so weak serving efficiency undercuts the "Chinese labs are hyper-efficient" story.
  • The unreleased frontier: benchmark against what labs hold internally, not just what they ship.
  • Model garden / tokenize-anywhere: keep multiple local and cloud models so no single disruption cuts off your intelligence.

Three Lessons for the Future of Computing

Lesson 1: Have a real AI safety posture

Jones says Kimi K3 marks the point where open-source models cross into being genuine cyber threats, and it only escalates from here. Unlike Fable, which is guard-railed (it won't help clone certain proprietary models and refuses hacking-style requests), Kimi K3 is happy to help on the hacking side. He urges: get the strongest model you can (Fable is a good example) and use it to audit your software from an adversarial posture, looking for leaks, malicious code, or even buggy code that exposes data. Build multiple layers of defense for your identity: strong passwords, two-factor authentication, moving from text codes to authenticator apps or fingerprint/USB keys. He recommends a family secret word so that if a voice or likeness is cloned in a ransom or wire-fraud scam, your family can verify it isn't really you. This is personal for him: his grandfather, who had dementia, lost money to wire fraud that was never recovered.

Lesson 2: Imagination and better questions are the new edge

With Kimi K3, Fable, 5.6 on the horizon, and Opus 5 coming, Jones argues you will be tested on your ability to ask the right questions. He describes someone who felt he had nothing interesting to give Fable; after brainstorming with someone who knew the model, he found three great use cases only Fable could do. The alpha is not in the predetermined use cases everyone already knows, it is in imagination married to AI, aimed at human connection and building incredible experiences. That means both touching grass and cultivating the imagination to ask genuinely interesting questions.

Lesson 3: Plan for government intervention and stay resilient

Jones expects governments to get more involved in restricting model distribution over the next six months, and not only the American government, there are rumors China may restrict certain tiers of open-source models. If scaling continues to Fable-level and perhaps Mythos-level open models by Christmas, expect policy surprises. His response: plan for a multi-model, diverse future. All of computing is trending toward being tokenized, so you want the ability to tokenize regardless of any single disruption (Anthropic, OpenAI, or a Chinese-model issue). Keep at least one primary model and one backup, whether via LM Studio locally or multiple cloud subscriptions, and be comfortable with each model's risk profile.

Practical Takeaways

  • Do not assume open weights means cheap: Kimi K3 needs a corporate-scale 64-accelerator-core footprint for top performance.
  • Account for token-inefficiency, not just per-token price, when estimating cost; Kimi burns more tokens per answer.
  • Benchmark against what labs hold internally; the released-vs-released comparison understates the closed-source lead (roughly 6-7 months).
  • Use your strongest available model to run an adversarial security audit of your own software.
  • Harden identity with layered defense: authenticator apps, fingerprint or USB keys, and a family secret word against voice/likeness cloning.
  • Cultivate better questions; brainstorm use cases with people who know the models to find non-obvious value.
  • Build a resilient "model garden": at least one primary and one backup model, local and cloud, so policy or provider disruptions don't cut you off.
  • Kimi K3 shines for coding and for tasks closed models refuse (e.g., cloning software), useful for affordably replacing SaaS solutions.
