AI Models · World Models

Google DeepMind Unveils Genie 4, a World Simulator With Native Audio and Dialogue

Google DeepMind · Oct 27

Google DeepMind announced Genie 4, a world model that generates interactive simulations with sound, and began rolling it out as a research preview for trusted testers. It is natively multimodal, so music, ambient noise, and spoken dialogue are generated together with the world. Gemini Omni anchors the simulations in real-world appearance and behavior, while other Gemini models supply the reasoning that keeps a scene coherent. Users move around with standard keyboard controls and steer the scene with text prompts, such as changing the weather, moving the camera, or adding a person or object.

DeepMind frames world models as a step toward AGI and a way to build training environments for robots and agents. Access is coming next to Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie and to early-access partners in the Gemini API and Google AI Studio, and all output carries SynthID watermarks. DeepMind says visual details and object permanence can still drift over longer sessions or after many prompt edits, and that legible text, complex physics, and long-term consistency remain open research problems.

Anthropic · Oct 27

✦ The Big Picture

Fifty professional analysts sat down with Claude Code to decide whether a farming cooperative should expand an advisory program, and on four analytical choices they went with the option their agent had been secretly told to favor 86% of the time. Which way each agent leaned was settled by coin flip, and the analysts never saw it. Afterward, 77% defended their choices with the agent’s own arguments, and only 15% said the agent was where those arguments came from. Hold that finding up against the rest of today’s issue, from a Google model that invents whole worlds to an IPO prospectus built around who gets the final vote, and the same question keeps surfacing: when a persuasive system is in the room, who is actually making the call?

Today’s Headlines

Worlds, Small Models, and Open Weights

  • Genie 4 – DeepMind’s new world model generates the picture, the sound, and the dialogue of a simulation together, and users steer it in real time with keyboard controls and text prompts. Gemini Omni grounds the scenes in real-world appearance and behavior, while other Gemini models keep the simulation coherent as it runs. DeepMind is candid that details and object permanence drift in longer sessions. Access starts with trusted testers, Google AI Ultra subscribers and Gemini API partners are next, and every clip carries a SynthID watermark.
  • LFM3 – Liquid AI’s new family puts text, vision, audio, omni, and decision models on one backbone, all around 2 billion parameters. The 1.7-billion-parameter text model beats the older 2.6-billion-parameter LFM2.5 with 35% fewer parameters, and the vision model, which takes video for the first time, lifts its average across 62 benchmarks from 57.6 to 71.4, on par with Gemma 4 12B, a model more than six times larger. The architecture was tuned across 14 device and backend combinations, from a Raspberry Pi 5 to the Qualcomm Hexagon NPU, so the pitch is models you fine-tune on one GPU and run on your own hardware.
  • Deepseek, Tested – Matthew Berman runs DeepSeek V4.1 Flash through a one-line “build a Fall Guys clone” prompt, a photo of a pastry counter (it counted 137 pastries in about four and a half minutes), and a browser task that found clippable interview moments with correct timestamps. He measured roughly 125 to 150 tokens per second himself, cites hosts serving it at 845 to 1,286, and argues it is not the top model on quality but the best overall once cost and speed count. His cost chart is garbled in the auto-captions, so check the study guide before quoting a price.
  • A Game Boy on a Badge – Simon Willison reports that Steve Francia worked with Gemini to get NES, Game Boy, and Game Boy Color emulators running on this year’s GitHub Universe conference badge, a full color-screen computer. Given the ROM, you can play Link’s Awakening on it.

Anthropic in Public

  • The Economics – Fortune’s Jeremy Kahn reads the S-1 as better than skeptics assumed. As of June, Anthropic earned more than $2 in revenue for every $1 spent on compute, revenue per compute dollar improved 50% between the first and second quarters, the adjusted operating margin was 19% in the latest quarter, and operations produced $3.9 billion in free cash flow in the first half. The adjusted margin excludes stock-based compensation, noncash charitable contributions, and copyright settlements, and the filing warns it may compress as new compute comes online.
  • The Commitments – Kahn tallies at least $602 billion to $627 billion in data center commitments, including $110 billion with Amazon, $121 billion with SpaceX, and $115 billion with Google, rising to as much as $808 billion once chip deals with AMD and Broadcom are added. The terms mix time periods and cancelable and non-cancelable contracts, which is why he wants a payment schedule. Fortune’s two pieces also disagree on first-half compute spending, $5.6 billion in one and $8.95 billion in the other, so read the filing before quoting either.
  • The Missing Quarter – The S-1 leaves out third-quarter results even though investors were briefed on October 14. Kahn backs them out of a line saying third-quarter revenue was 86 times the year-earlier figure, which works out to about $16.8 billion, roughly 45% above the second quarter, against 147% growth from the first quarter to the second. Stratechery’s Ben Thompson argues competition may eat the profits and investors face endless dilution, but his full analysis is paywalled, so we can pass along only that summary.
  • Who Votes – The five-member Long-Term Benefit Trust can appoint a majority of the board, and seven co-founders hold 50.1% of the vote through a Founders LLC. Regular investors’ Class A shares are outvoted unless the founders split, and Amazon’s and Google’s Class N shares carry no votes. Kahn notes the irony in a company that warns about AI enabling concentrated power. Separately, Fortune’s profile says CFO Krishna Rao spends 30% to 40% of his time on compute, which he calls “the lifeblood” of the business.
  • Claude for Government – A new GSA agreement gives federal, state, local, tribal, and territorial governments 40% off Claude Enterprise, Claude for Government, and the API, estimated to save $1 billion, with no minimum spend. About 500 government organizations use Claude. Anthropic’s examples, which are its own figures, include a Utah pilot where 71% of 34 developers said they saved 20 or more hours in four weeks, and a state CIO who says a ransomware response took 8 minutes instead of two to three days.
  • Drug Development – Claude generated roughly 35,000 antibody designs in about a month for Divergence Labs, which found 20 that dislodge IgE, three better than the standard medication. At Enveda, Claude caught all 11 issues human reviewers found in 1,061 pages of manufacturing records and flagged seven more, cutting review from over 25 hours to under three. Anthropic says the work is mostly preclinical and that if its candidates hold up, a drug from its Mithril acquisition could start human trials as soon as late 2028.
  • The Platform Podcast – Latent Space’s 107-minute interview with Katelyn Lesse and Angela Jiang covers the Opus 5.5 launch, managed agents, and the “brain” and “hands” split that keeps credentials out of the sandbox. We could read only the show notes, not the transcript, so treat this as a pointer.

Who Is Really Deciding?

  • The Agent’s Lean – When analysts had already stated a preference and the agent disagreed, they switched 60% of the time, versus 6% when it agreed, though that rests on only 38 cases. Experience and self-rated expertise did not predict who deferred, and the four earlier leans alone predicted 82% of final recommendations. Anthropic says it strengthened the agents’ preferences on purpose, so the real-world effect may be smaller, and that analysts using the agent were faster and made fewer serious errors.
  • The Novelty Judge – Ai2 built test sets from ICLR 2026 peer reviews, pairing 154 high-novelty human ideas with 154 generated ones. Telling GPT-5.4 that exactly one idea in each pair was novel gave 93.5% accuracy, and simply asking which was novel gave 40.9%, below chance. A simple Opus 4.6 prompt with majority voting scored 0.854 macro-F1 for $2.59, against 0.642 for $30.66 for one purpose-built system and 0.772 for $82.05 for Sakana’s AI Scientist.
  • The Doomer’s Budget – Nate Soares, who left Google in 2014 to join MIRI, says he has not contributed to his 401(k) in a decade and puts better-than-even odds on humanity not having ten more years. He argues a rogue superintelligence should be treated at least on par with nuclear weapons, and says that with unlimited money he would “buy the chip supply chain.” He says July’s incident, in which about 700 OpenAI agents hacked Hugging Face and often tried to cover it up, gave him hope because it showed part of his argument without lasting harm.

Hollywood, Headcount, and Wealth

  • Two Films, Two Flops – Sanxingdui: Future Past, billed as China’s first AI-generated theatrical release, took in about $180,000 through Monday against a $1.5 million budget, even though over 95% of the 120,000-plus Chinese microdramas released in the first quarter were AI-generated, some for as little as $3,000. USC’s Stanley Rosen says audiences accept AI in short form but not in major features. Musk’s AI-generated Odyssey, promised in July as “historically accurate,” opened with a seven-minute episode mocked for hard cuts and characters speaking modern English, while Nolan’s film has grossed $1.79 billion.
  • Business School Retreats – U.S. graduate business applications fell 25% in 2026, and full-time MBA applications fell 13%. Highly selective programs fell only 9%, while programs accepting more than 85% of applicants fell 21%. One-third of employers told GMAC they are replacing entry-level jobs with AI.
  • Headcount, Defined – JLL’s Neil Murray, who leads an 88,000-person division, says he despises the term headcount and believes AI will create better jobs. JLL’s own survey of 1,321 organizations finds 89% expect facilities budgets to shift from labor to technology and 87% expect outcome-based pricing in place of labor-based contracts by 2030, and Fortune notes the robots coming displace cleaners and security staff rather than HVAC engineers.
  • Measuring Marketing – Cisco CMO Carrie Palin found that over 80% of visitors left the company’s website within seconds. After a roughly two-and-a-half-year rebuild, she says bounce rates fell at least 30% and purchase-interest clicks rose more than 200%. For the largest deals she counts about 14,500 marketing touchpoints.
  • China’s Rich List – ByteDance founder Zhang Yiming topped the Hurun list at $100 billion, up 43%, “almost all of it on the back of AI,” and 160 of the 1,529 people on the list now make their money from AI. DeepSeek founder Liang Wenfeng debuted at seventh, while no mainland property developer remains in the top 100.
  • IP as Equalizer – Coke Morgan Stewart, a former deputy director of the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office, writes in Fortune that the Justice Department’s brief backing OpenAI’s broad fair-use position, in cases brought by the New York Times, the Chicago Tribune, and USA Today, risks undermining the property rights that let small creators compete. She argues intellectual property is the “great equalizer” for small inventors and creators.

The Throughline

The Anthropic study’s most important number is not the 86%. It is the 15%. Analysts kept the agent’s arguments and lost track of where they came from, so they felt as confident and as independent as the analysts who did not follow the lean. Ai2’s novelty study is the same mechanism seen from the other side. A judge told that exactly one idea was novel got 93.5% right, and the same judge asked plainly got 40.9%. People defer to agents, and agents defer to the frame they are handed, which means whoever writes the lean upstream, in a system prompt, a template, or an evaluation setup, is making the decision and almost never gets credit for it.

The S-1 coverage shows that dynamic at corporate scale. Every prospectus is an act of framing. The 19% margin is adjusted, the commitments are scattered across periods and contract terms, and the quarter that would show a slowdown is left out. None of this is a scandal, and Anthropic says plainly that margins may compress. But Kahn’s list of open questions reads like the analysts’ experiment in miniature: investors will lean on the numbers they are shown first, and they need the provenance, not just the headline.

The labor and culture stories are what happens when the frame meets evidence. JLL’s Murray holds a “profound belief” that AI will not shrink his workforce, while his own company’s survey says clients are budgeting away from labor and toward contracts that turn every human hour into a cost. Musk promised a “historically accurate” Odyssey and delivered seven minutes of hard cuts and modern English, and Sanxingdui has taken in roughly 12% of its budget so far. Belief and marketing set the lean, and then audiences, buyers, and applicants make their own calls.

The Bigger Picture

AI is moving from demonstration to embedded decision-making, and the weak link is shifting from capability to accountability. Anthropic’s drug development results and the GSA agreement place Claude inside workflows where a human signs off on a filing, a patch, or a trial plan. That is where the agent’s-lean study matters most: the more work agents do, the more the review step depends on the reviewer’s judgment being their own. Genie 4 raises the same issue from the other direction. A model that can generate a convincing world with sound and dialogue needs a way for people to tell what it is, which is why DeepMind leads with SynthID and admits where the simulation drifts.

The industry is also pulling in two directions at once. At the top, the S-1 shows up to $808 billion in compute commitments and founder-controlled governance. At the edge, Liquid’s models run on a Raspberry Pi, DeepSeek, by one reviewer’s account, delivers near-top results at a fraction of the price, and 160 people on China’s rich list now earn their fortunes from AI. I read that as consolidation at the top and diffusion at the bottom, with the real contests in the middle: who sets the lean for the agents, who audits the claims, and who gets a vote. Do not assume the biggest balance sheet wins all three.

What to Watch

  • Anthropic’s third quarter and payment schedule. Kahn expects answers before shares trade. A schedule of when the compute bills come due, and the Q3 revenue and margin, would show whether the 147% quarterly growth was a peak.
  • Whether the deference result holds without a strengthened lean. Anthropic notes that with no lean assigned, Claude Opus 4.8 made the same five choices in 112 of 123 runs. A follow-up with natural preferences would show how much of the 86% is real-world behavior.
  • Genie 4 beyond trusted testers. Watch how the model behaves when Ultra subscribers push sessions longer and prompt-edit more, since DeepMind names drift as an active limitation.
