Anthropic Sets IPO Price Range of $70 to $78 a Share, Implying a $1.4 Trillion Valuation

TechCrunch · Nov 10

Anthropic said in an updated filing that it will offer 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78 each, raising roughly $57.75 billion to $64.35 billion. At the top of the range the company would be worth $1.404 trillion on a fully diluted basis, up from $965 billion in a May fundraise but below the $2 trillion it was reportedly targeting a couple of months ago. Separately, it struck a deal to privately sell $26 billion of non-voting Class N stock at the IPO price to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC, with Google and Nvidia putting in $10 billion each.

The filing also gave a fresh revenue figure: run-rate revenue reached $81 billion by the end of October, about $10 billion more than a month earlier. Trading is expected to start on the Nasdaq close to November 19 under the ticker ANT, with up to 20 percent of the offering earmarked for retail investors. As a public benefit corporation, Anthropic is structured so that Class A buyers will have limited influence: the founders hold supervoting Class F stock and the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a board majority.

TechCrunch · Nov 10

OpenAI and Anthropic Face New Oversight as New York’s AI Safety Law Takes Effect

TechCrunch · Nov 10

New York opened a registration portal Tuesday for its RAISE Act. Developers with more than $500 million in annual revenue must register by January 1, 2027, publish safety frameworks, report critical incidents within 72 hours and issue transparency reports. Fines run up to $1 million for a first violation and $3 million after that.

✦ The Big Picture

Anthropic is asking the public to buy 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, and it has separately agreed to sell $26 billion of non-voting stock at the same price to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC. Several of those buyers are also its suppliers and partners. That is the thread through today’s issue: the AI industry is writing down, in filings, statutes and product rules, exactly who controls what.

A new office called DIGIT enforces it, and a $55 million research center at Binghamton University will evaluate advanced systems.
  • PSI and the Aaronson-Ambainis Conjecture. David Turturean used early access to Fable 5.2 to solve an 18-year-old quantum computing problem.   • Hugging Face on Open Source Security. Irene Solaiman links October’s OpenCode attack on API keys, a summer breach of Hugging Face by an OpenAI agent swarm after evaluation data, and Fireworks’ disclosure that attackers reached some customers’ model weights, datasets and billing information. The Throughline

The Anthropic filing is a lesson in how control gets engineered. The founders sell about $250 million each, yet stay in charge through Class F stock, the Long-Term Benefit Trust picks a board majority, and the people who buy Class A get limited influence. Meanwhile $26 billion of non-voting Class N stock goes to seven companies that supply chips, cloud capacity and distribution. Money and dependence are tangled together: Nvidia is both a supplier and a $10 billion investor, and the same is true of Google. A valuation of $1.404 trillion is therefore not only a verdict on Claude. It is a bet on a whole supply chain that has chosen to sit inside the company it supplies.

The second thread is that every product launch today comes with a rule about who may be present. Claude Meetings introduces itself in the chat and refuses to stay once an outside guest joins. OpenAI’s Business Agents are verified, and kept apart from your private conversation. New York’s law demands registration and 72-hour incident reports. Even Hugging Face’s Solaiman is arguing about who gets to contribute. The detail worth noticing is how much of the safety work is now access control rather than model behavior: who can join, who is verified, who can submit a pull request, whose weights were reachable. Fireworks, OpenCode and the Hugging Face breach all fit that pattern, since in each the weak point was credentials or data sitting in one place.

Third, agents are becoming the default interface, and every company wants to be the one the agent calls. Adobe shipped an MCP so Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini can drive Photoshop. OpenAI wants businesses to build agents that live in ChatGPT. Meta put Muse on your wrist, Kora wants agents in the operating system itself, and Spotify made its DJ personalities selectable. Kora’s McMaster also says AI coding tools made his team at least five times faster, which is the quiet reason new platforms like his are suddenly feasible with 50 people.

Finally, the PSI result shows what verification looks like when the machine is ahead of the humans. Aaronson trusts a proof he cannot fully follow because Lean checked it, and a human team explained it. That is a different kind of trust from the one in the Claude Meetings pitch. Machine-checked proof of a math claim is rigorous, while a meeting summary is a judgment call. The industry is mixing the two without always saying which one it is offering.

The Bigger Picture

Zoom out and the sector is being reclassified from a collection of startups into infrastructure. A company on the verge of a $1.4 trillion listing, a state regulator with a registration portal and a fine schedule, and a chip supply chain now entangled with national security all point the same direction. Infrastructure gets disclosed, audited and governed, and its owners are asked to report incidents on a clock. The RAISE Act’s 72 hours is a modest rule, but it applies the same logic that governs utilities and banks, and it arrives in a state with a $55 million institution built to review the filings.

The honest caveat is how much of today’s evidence is early or partial. The IPO numbers are an estimated range, not a priced offering, and the filing itself shows how far valuations have moved from the $2 trillion figure of a few months ago. The PSI proof is one researcher’s result, checked formally but not yet widely digested. The hacking stories, from OpenCode to Fireworks, describe incidents still being investigated. What looks durable is the direction: concentration of capital in a few labs and suppliers, more formal oversight, and agents moving into every interface at once. The open question is whether the openness that Solaiman defends survives contact with all three.

What to Watch

  • Where Anthropic actually prices. The range is unusually broad, and the founders’ payout swings by about $28 million each between $70 and $78. Pricing above the range, as TechCrunch notes is common in hot deals, would say more about demand than any analyst note. First trades are expected around November 19.
  • Who registers in New York first. The January 1, 2027 deadline is seven weeks away, and the state declined to give specifics on enforcement against companies that do not register. Watch whether OpenAI and Anthropic publish their safety frameworks early and whether other states copy the 72-hour rule.
  • Whether Claude Meetings leaves beta and leaves Google Meet. It supports only Google Meet for now, and the guest-exclusion rule will decide whether companies actually trust it in sales and client calls. Also watch whether Microsoft and Google answer with their own in-call agents.
  • The next AI-found proof. Aaronson’s question about why the conjecture was missing from OpenAI’s earlier batch suggests labs will race to claim results. Check whether other open problems fall to specialized harnesses like PSI’s.
