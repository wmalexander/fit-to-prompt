Anthropic Ships Claude Opus 5 at Half the Price of Its Own Frontier Model

Anthropic · Jul 24

Opus 5 lands at $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens, unchanged from Opus 4.8, and Anthropic's claim is that it delivers something close to Claude Fable 5 intelligence at half the price. The benchmark table is unusually lopsided. On Frontier-Bench v0.1 it surpasses every other model and more than doubles Opus 4.8 at a lower cost per task. On CursorBench 3.2 it lands within 0.5 percent of Fable 5's peak at half the cost per task. On ARC-AGI 3 it scores three times the next-best model. On OSWorld 2.0 it beats Fable 5's best result at just over a third of the cost.

The tier improvements are where the agent story lives. New beta features let tools change mid-conversation and let the model fall back automatically to an alternative when a safety classifier intervenes, both of which matter far more to a process that runs for hours than to a chat turn. Life-sciences gains are concrete: 10.2 percentage points over Opus 4.8 on organic chemistry, 7.7 on protein prediction. A Fast mode runs roughly 2.5 times the default speed at double the base price.

Anthropic also publishes the number it did not have to. Opus 5 scores 2.3 on automated behavioral audits, which the company describes as the lowest of recent models, and it still trails Mythos 5 on cybersecurity exploitation tasks. Read that next to Dario Amodei's open-weights essay elsewhere in this issue, which argues that every sufficiently capable model should face mandatory pre-release testing for exactly these categories. The company is making its own case by showing its own scorecard.

Dario Amodei: We Never Asked for a Ban on Open Weights

Anthropic · Jul 27

Amodei writes to correct what he says is a misreading of Anthropic's position: the company has never advocated banning open-weights models, and models without dangerous capabilities provide real public value. His two actual concerns are authoritarian governments outbuilding the US for military or population-control purposes, and powerful models being turned to cyber or biological attack. His prescription is three specific measures rather than a ban. Restrict chip sales to China and pursue smuggling, because limited domestic chip capacity is the binding constraint on Chinese frontier training. Treat industrial-scale distillation as a policy problem, since distillation lets a chip-constrained lab exceed what its silicon should allow. And require every sufficiently capable model, open or closed, to pass safety testing for cyber, bio, and alignment risk before release. He grants parts of the industry's open letter, including the competition and customer-control arguments, but disputes the claim that open models are inherently safer, citing attacker-defender asymmetry in biology. The question, he argues, should be settled by testing rather than assumed in advance.

Cognizant Trains 30,000 Associates on Claude and Embeds It Across Its Platforms

Anthropic · Jul 27

Cognizant becomes a Global Premier Partner in the Claude Partner Network and is wiring Claude into Flowsource, Neuro AI Engineering, and Neuro IT Ops. Flowsource now runs Claude Code alongside human engineers in its Spec-Driven Development module, steered by project specs, coding standards, and architectural blueprints. The client numbers are the interesting part: a customer experience portal for a global manufacturer delivered in six months; an agentic contract-intelligence system for a biopharmaceutical company cutting review time by up to 40 percent while pushing extraction accuracy above 88 percent; a risk-navigation tool that turns hours of underwriter work into minutes and saves roughly eight hours per person per week. Cognizant's CEO frames the whole thing bluntly: "AI capability is rising faster than enterprises can absorb it, and that gap is the defining problem of this moment." Thirty thousand trained associates is one answer to an absorption problem.

Steve Yegge on Why Gas Town Fell Apart: "Just Two More Things"

Simon Willison's Weblog · Aug 4

Simon Willison quotes Steve Yegge on the collapse of Gas Town, a system built to be reusable that in practice only ever built itself. It worked through Opus 4.6 and then came apart at the seams with 4.7. Yegge names the specific behavior that broke it: a "just two more things" tic that kept the model from ever converging on being ready to do real work, so it went on modifying Gas Town instead of finishing anything. Other problems contributed, but that is the one he identifies as the breaking point, and it persisted in the versions that followed. It is a useful counterweight to a benchmark table. A model can be measurably better at nearly everything and still be unusable for a long-running agentic loop because of one convergence habit, and no evaluation suite in this issue reports on that.

Fortune · Aug 4

    Anthropic published a benchmark table today that reads like a victory lap and a confession at the same time. Today's issue is what happens when a lab argues for a rule and then publishes its own report card under it, while the rest of the market spends the day pricing chips, listings, and oil.

    The Model

    • Opus 5 is a price argument as much as a capability one. It ships at $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output, identical to Opus 4.8, and Anthropic's framing is frontier intelligence close to Fable 5 at half the cost. The specific claims: more than double Opus 4.8 on Frontier-Bench v0.1 at lower cost per task, within 0.5 percent of Fable 5's peak on CursorBench 3.2 at half the cost per task, three times the next-best score on ARC-AGI 3, roughly 1.5x the next-best pass rate on Zapier AutomationBench, and a win over Fable 5's best OSWorld 2.0 result at just over a third of the cost.
    • The new features are aimed at long-running agents, not chat. Two beta additions matter disproportionately: tools can now change mid-conversation, and the model can fall back automatically to an alternative when a safety classifier intervenes. Neither does much for a single question. Both are load-bearing for a process that runs for hours and cannot afford to die on a tool swap or a refusal.
    • The domain gains are concrete. 10.2 percentage points over Opus 4.8 on organic chemistry, 7.7 on protein prediction. A Fast mode runs about 2.5 times default speed at double the base price. Availability spans Claude.ai, Claude Code, Claude Cowork, and the API.
    • The safety numbers are in the same post. 2.3 on automated behavioral audits, described as the lowest of recent models, and a standing gap behind Mythos 5 on cyber exploitation. Publishing the weak row alongside the strong ones is a choice, and it is the same choice the open-weights essay is asking regulators to make mandatory.

Everything else in the essay follows from that premise. If the premise weakens, the policy weakens with it.

He rejects the assertion that open models are inherently safer, citing attacker-defender asymmetry in biology, and argues the question should be settled by rigorous testing rather than assumed in advance.

    The Throughline

    The interesting thing about today is that Anthropic argued both sides of its own case, in public, within four days. The open-weights essay says the industry should not be trusted to self-certify: every sufficiently capable model, whoever ships it, should have to pass cyber, biological, and alignment testing before release. The Opus 5 announcement then publishes exactly that kind of result, including the unflattering rows. Opus 5 scores 2.3 on automated behavioral audits, the lowest of recent models, and it remains behind Mythos 5 on cyber exploitation tasks. That is a company demonstrating the regime it wants imposed, using itself as the worked example. It is also, conveniently, a regime that a well-resourced lab with an existing evaluation pipeline can absorb more easily than a small open-weights team can.

    The chip premise is where the argument is most exposed. Amodei's three policies all reduce to one claim: China cannot train frontier models without US silicon, so control the silicon and control the frontier. Today's wire is four separate stories poking at that claim from different angles. CXMT is closing on Samsung and SK Hynix in memory. Huawei's chief scientist is arguing Nvidia is approaching a physical ceiling, which would mean the gap stops widening on its own. Chinese labs are producing enough pressure that Bloomberg is describing a "death zone" for US model makers in the middle of the market. None of that is proof, and all of it is behind a paywall, but the direction of every one of those headlines runs against the premise. If domestic capacity arrives sooner than the policy assumes, export controls become a delay rather than a constraint, and delay is a much weaker thing to build a safety regime on.

    Then there is Yegge, who is not writing about policy at all and who quietly undermines the whole evaluation frame. Gas Town died of a behavioral tic. Not a capability shortfall, not a safety failure, not a benchmark regression: a model that would not stop adding two more things. Opus 5's headline improvements are aimed at precisely the long-running agentic workloads where that failure mode is fatal, and its published numbers say nothing about it. The whole apparatus being proposed as the gate on model releases measures capability and measures harm. It does not measure whether the thing converges. Cognizant's CEO says capability is rising faster than enterprises can absorb it. Yegge's post is a specific example of why: the gap is not only training and change management, it is that the useful properties of an agent are not the properties anyone is scoring.

    Put the three together and the shape of the day is a lab that has gotten very good at measuring what it can measure, arguing that everyone should be measured that way, while the two most consequential uncertainties, whether the chip constraint holds and whether the models actually finish anything, sit outside the measurement entirely.

    The Bigger Picture

    The last two years of AI governance have run on an assumption that is starting to show its seams: that frontier capability lives inside a small number of legible, jurisdictionally reachable organizations, and that a government wanting to steer AI can do so by regulating them. Every serious proposal, including the one Amodei published this week, inherits that assumption. Mandatory pre-release testing works if there is a release event, a releaser, and a jurisdiction. Chip controls work if compute is the binding constraint and the chokepoint is Western. Both are true today. Neither is a law of nature.

    What today's board shows is the assumption being tested from several directions at once. Memory manufacturing gaps narrowing, a Huawei scientist arguing Nvidia is nearing a physical wall, Chinese labs squeezing the US middle market, and capital reorganizing itself around Hong Kong listings rather than US ones. None of those is a frontier-model story. All of them are stories about whether the infrastructure underneath the governance assumption stays where the governance assumes it is. If it moves, the mandatory-testing proposal does not become wrong so much as it becomes local: a rule that binds American labs and describes nothing about the models most of the world can download.

    The second thing worth naming is the widening gap between what the industry measures and what it sells. Opus 5's card is a genuinely impressive document, and it is a document about a model's performance on tests. The thing being sold is an agent that runs for hours inside somebody's business process. Cognizant's numbers are the closest thing in today's issue to evidence about that second thing, and they are vendor-supplied case studies: 40 percent off contract review, eight hours a week per underwriter. Yegge's is the closest thing to evidence against it, and it is one developer's post-mortem. Between an evaluation suite that does not test convergence and a market that buys on evaluation suites, there is room for a lot of disappointed enterprises, and Cognizant is quite explicitly building a business in that room.

    What to Watch

    • Whether independent evaluations reproduce Opus 5's cost-per-task claims. Nearly every headline number in the announcement is a ratio against cost, not a raw score: half the cost per task on CursorBench, a third the cost on OSWorld, lower cost per task on Frontier-Bench. Ratios are more fragile than scores, because they depend on token efficiency under someone else's harness. If third-party runs show the raw capability but not the economics, the pitch changes from "frontier at half price" to "frontier."
    • Whether anyone proposes a convergence benchmark. Yegge's failure mode is specific, reproducible in principle, and completely unmeasured. The gap between "scores well on agentic benchmarks" and "finishes a long-running job without being babysat" is now the most commercially important unmeasured property in the field. The first lab to publish a credible number for it will reset how these announcements are read.
    • Whether the chip-constraint premise survives the next two quarters of Chinese manufacturing news. CXMT in memory and Huawei's ceiling argument are both leading indicators, not conclusions. Watch for independent confirmation of Chinese process-node progress rather than vendor claims. Anthropic's entire policy proposal is downstream of that one question, and so is the case for treating export controls as a safety instrument rather than a trade one.
