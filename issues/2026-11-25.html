✦ The Big Picture

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83, debuted on Nasdaq at $111.50, and by midday Tuesday was trading as low as $76, down nearly 12% from Monday’s close. That is less than a week from first trade to below the offer price, and TechCrunch notes that the AI industry has been treating the stock as a barometer for how public investors value the whole sector. Nvidia fell more than 7% the same day, giving back part of the 30%-plus jump it posted Monday on Jensen Huang’s reinstatement. Two of the biggest AI stories of the month are now trading in the same direction, and the rest of today’s issue explains why that is uncomfortable.

Today’s Headlines

A note on sources: Bloomberg blocked automated access when this issue was built, so its six items are summarized from published headlines only. The OpenAI math preprint and the Claude Code release page were summarized from their titles and release notes.

Nvidia After the Comeback

  • The legal bill is arriving. Yale’s Jeffrey Sonnenfeld says the directors who brought Huang back face a “historic level of individual liability.” TechCrunch counts five suits, all filed in Northern California: a class action, a shareholder derivative suit alleging the board ignored red flags about Alice Huang, and three more on November 18. Sonnenfeld predicts about 30 eventually. Nvidia is also juggling a Justice Department antitrust investigation, a reported SEC probe, a possible FBI review and a California subpoena.
  • A pardon is “an option.” Trump told the New York Post that pardoning Huang is “always an option, I guess, but I don’t think it’ll be necessary.” Huang is scheduled to testify at a public Senate Banking Committee hearing next week, and Trump said he wants him to “just tell the truth.”
  • The all-hands was an apology tour. CNBC reported Huang told staff “I hurt all of you” and that he is in therapy. He said the code of conduct, which he largely devised, still applies, but that violating it “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” as Wired reported it. He also thanked the administration for quick access to intelligence that the Wall Street Journal says helped the board judge the national security risk of reinstating him as low.
  • No succession plan, on purpose. Huang reportedly told staff a plan would create unnecessary hierarchy. Nvidia has five executive officers and Huang says he has about 60 direct reports. CFO Colette Kress served as interim CEO for 12 days, which is the only live test of the alternative.

Agents, Privacy and Retail

  • Agents fail more often than the marketing implies. ShellBench, built with researchers from the OpenClaw Foundation, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley and drawing on more than 10 million anonymized agent interactions, found top models from OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta failed more than a third of the time. Across all models, 15.7% of attempts broke at least one safety requirement. The top scorer, OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra, got all three runs of the same task right less than half the time. Agents failed to account for a child’s school pickup 79.5% of the time and failed to withhold a sensitive bank upload token in 18.6% of attempts on one task.
  • Walmart is letting Meta’s Muse shop for you. Customers sign in to Walmart inside Muse and the agent finds, compares and builds a cart, with spending limits and approval-before-purchase options. Muse does not see shopping history. The connection runs through Walmart’s Agent Gateway, launched in October and planned to open to more AI platforms, while Amazon has taken steps to stop third-party agents from buying for customers.
  • Maple’s pitch is verifiability, not trust. The startup closed a $5.3 million seed round led by Epic Ventures and runs its AI in trusted execution environments with open-source code, so users can check what runs on its servers. It launched a desktop agent called Maple Work and plans a cloud agent next and a Series A in the first half of 2027.

Chips, Models and Research

  • Korea’s one-champion strategy. Fortune reports Rebellions, valued at $2.3 billion, is backed by both Samsung and SK Hynix, with SK Telecom and KT asked to become customers. A $400 million round in March was co-led by the Korea National Growth Fund. CEO Sunghyun Park says that backing makes it “more powerful compared to any other player in the United States,” though shelved data center IPOs will test the confidence.
  • Gemini 4 Neon. Latent Space says Neon beats Astra and Fable on LiveBench and cut one TypeScript-to-Rust benchmark runtime by 48.6%, and that sentiment about Google DeepMind has reversed from three months ago. The same roundup relays an unconfirmed claim of a VP vs. VNP proof posted by Anthropic and a counter-release from OpenAI, with the caveat that neither lab announced a model.
  • PenGPT. A 6M-parameter model writes print and cursive in a new person’s hand after seeing a few of their words, and a 54M-parameter model sketches from text. The data was 495,590 words from 1,255 writers and 477,670 sketches. The author says the post was written by Claude and that he will rewrite it himself.
  • Hacked tankers. The Wall Street Journal reports U.S. officials suspect Iranian hackers in as many as 50 attacks on oil, gas and chemical ships, possibly through a bug dubbed FortiBleed in Fortinet routers that an FBI and Coast Guard alert flagged in September.
  • Developer tools. Claude Code v2.1.332 adds skills from MCP servers (beta) and a hook event that can refuse a skill’s inline shell commands.
  • On the wire. Bloomberg headlines cover Malaysia tightening controls amid U.S. AI chip curbs, key-man risk at Nvidia, Alibaba exploring a yuan and dollar bond sale, a guilty verdict for Huawei on U.S. charges, a U.S. critique of Australia’s social media law, and a strong year for the won on the AI chip boom. OpenAI also posted a preprint titled “The Matrix Multiplication Exponent Is Nine Fourths.”

The Throughline

Sonnenfeld ties the day together in a way the other stories only imply. He argues Anthropic’s sinking shares reflect a growing view that China’s open source models are in part copies of top U.S. labs’ work, made with high-end Nvidia chips, and he notes Nvidia has denied that its most advanced chips were smuggled. Whether or not you accept his causal story, the market data he cites lines up: Nvidia up more than 30% Monday, down more than 7% Tuesday, and Anthropic below its offer price. Investors seem to be pricing the same unresolved risk from two directions.

The ShellBench result is the quiet counterweight. The industry is selling personal agents to millions of people, and Meta says more than 10 million set up Muse within six weeks. The benchmark says the best models are inconsistent and sometimes unsafe in exactly the messy territory consumers hand them. Walmart’s spending limits and approval gates, and Maple’s verifiable privacy, read as admissions that the guardrails matter more than the model.

Huang’s management story belongs here too. A company run with 60 direct reports and no succession plan worked until the founder’s personal conduct became a market event. His own all-hands answer, that the code of conduct still applies but circumstances matter, is a policy position that will be tested in a Senate hearing next week.

The Bigger Picture

The AI trade has been priced as one story, with Nvidia as the supplier and a handful of labs as the customers. This week showed it can come apart. A leadership reversal moved the whole market up on Monday, and a lab’s first week as a public company moved sentiment down on Tuesday. If the sector now trades on a few names, then governance and politics at those names matter as much as model quality.

Rebellions points to the longer arc. Korea is concentrating Samsung’s foundries, SK Hynix’s memory and state money behind one inference-focused chip company so it does not depend on Nvidia. Add the hacked tankers and the Malaysian chip controls, and AI infrastructure is now a national security question, not just a procurement one. The honest unknown is whether sovereign champions can beat an incumbent that, for now, is also fighting a pile of lawsuits.

What to Watch

  • Anthropic’s stock. TechCrunch says continued weakness could pressure private AI valuations and other IPO plans. Watch whether the price recovers toward $83 or keeps sliding.
  • Huang’s Senate Banking testimony. It is scheduled for next week, with closed House Intelligence, Foreign Affairs and China Select Committee hearings to follow.
  • The lawsuit count. Five suits are filed, and Sonnenfeld expects far more. Each new filing tests whether the board’s reliance on government assurances holds up.
