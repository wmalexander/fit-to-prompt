California Will Run on Claude, at Half Price, Under a New Anthropic Deal

TechCrunch · Jun 29

Governor Gavin Newsom and Anthropic have struck an agreement that gives California's state agencies, and its local governments, access to Claude at roughly half the usual price, bundled with training and support so public employees can use it to draft documents, analyze data, and field constituent work. It is one of the largest government adoptions of a frontier model to date, and it routes the most populous state in the country through a single company's AI.

The deal lands in a week when Washington was busy deciding who gets to use the closed frontier at all. California's move is the mirror image: not gatekeeping access, but subsidizing it at scale, and betting that the productivity payoff outweighs the dependence it creates. For Anthropic, half price buys something a press release can't, a captive public-sector footprint that competitors will now have to dislodge.

OKX Wants AI Agents to Hire, Pay, and Rate Each Other

TechCrunch · Jun 30

Crypto exchange OKX is building a marketplace where AI agents can commission work from one another, settle payments autonomously on-chain, and accumulate portable reputations, an "economy of agents" that treats software the way a gig platform treats freelancers. It is the most literal version yet of the agentic-future pitch: not humans directing one model, but models transacting with each other, with crypto rails as the settlement layer. Whether that is a glimpse of efficiency or a new surface for runaway automation is exactly the question it raises.

TechCrunch · Jun 29

Samsung and SK Hynix plan to pour more than $518 billion into four new memory-fabrication plants in southwestern Korea, a national bet to keep the country at the center of the AI hardware boom as demand for high-bandwidth memory strains supply. ✦ The Big Picture

California just bet its entire government on Claude, at half price, in the same week the Pentagon branded Anthropic a "supply-chain risk" and Washington kept its hand on the switch for OpenAI's GPT-5.6. One government is subsidizing the closed frontier as fast as it can; another is rationing it. And underneath both, a free model you can download proved again that the thing being guarded may not be the scarce thing at all. The story this week isn't any single model. It's that nobody, in government or industry, can agree on what a frontier model even is: a public utility, a weapon, or a commodity already slipping out the door.

▶ Listen to the Digest ~8 min

Government Can't Decide What AI Is

  • California subsidizes Claude while the Pentagon blacklists it. Governor Newsom's deal gives state agencies and local governments Claude at roughly half price, bundled with training, with Newsom framing it as augmentation: "AI should not replace the human work of government; it should help our workers move faster." The whiplash is that the same company is, federally, a "supply-chain risk", the DoD rejected Anthropic's safeguards against surveillance and autonomous-weapons use and contracted OpenAI instead. California's CIO said the federal designation "just didn't come up." Two arms of government, opposite verdicts, same vendor.
  • Washington is rationing GPT-5.6, and the case for it is thin. Matt Maher walks through the first real instance of the U.S. controlling who can use a frontier model, and pushes back hard: demanding a model be un-jailbreakable, he argues, is like telling Microsoft it can't ship software until it proves there will never be a bug. His sharper point is geopolitical, "these are the dumbest frontier models we will ever see again", so freezing the weakest future version while DeepSeek sits maybe six months behind hands the lead away.
  • The thing being guarded has a real capability behind it. Maher concedes the milestone underneath the politics: Anthropic's internal "Mythos" model (publicly "Fable 5"), pointed at open-source infrastructure under Project Glass Wing, helped surface roughly 10,000 severe vulnerabilities, and an NSA red team verified it could break into their own systems quickly. The capability is real; whether export controls and approved-user lists are the right tool for it is the open question.

The Open Frontier Keeps Showing Up

  • Opus finally has "real competition," with a catch. IndyDevDan's verdict on the open-weights field: "GLM-5.2 is the better model, but MiniMax-M3 is the better deal", GLM lands a top-five intelligence score, MiniMax is roughly 5x cheaper. But he refuses the "GLM replaces Opus" hype: "workhorse models call tools like Opus, but they don't ship like Opus," and the gap widens on long-horizon work. His framework is a tiered "model stack," each tier down costing about 5x less for a small capability drop.
  • The open models are landing everywhere at once. Quesma calls Qwen 3.6 27B "the first local model that actually makes sense as a general intelligence," running ~30 tokens/sec on a MacBook. Simon Willison reviewed Ornith-1.0, an MIT-licensed family (9B-397B) from the obscure DeepReinforce, built on Gemma 4 and Qwen 3.5, that drew his signature pelican at 103 tokens/sec. LongCat-2.0, meanwhile, is a 1.6-trillion-parameter model trained entirely on Chinese silicon. The frontier is leaking in four directions simultaneously.
  • And owning open weights is now a resilience strategy. IndyDevDan's most pointed argument comes after "Fable" got pulled: three of his four reference models can't be switched off by anyone, so depending on a closed model that can "rug pull" is now a business risk, not just a cost question.

The Value Moved to the Harness

  • Nate B. Jones names the shift: the intelligence war is becoming a context war. When frontier intelligence slows even for a few weeks, he argues, the edge stops being the newest model and becomes "the context that makes any good model useful", which message matters, which file is current, what the team decided. He reads Apple's Siri, Claude's new Slack integration, and GLM-5.2 as the same story: a model doesn't need to be very smart to be very useful if it's wired into the right context.
  • Spotify is living proof at 20 million lines. Niklas Gustavsson says ~73% of Spotify's pull requests are now AI-authored, against ~4,500 production deployments a day and roughly 2,900 engineers, a 75%+ jump in PR frequency with quality metrics held neutral. The unlock wasn't a smarter model; it was the harness. Their HONK platform runs the Claude Agent SDK in Kubernetes pods, and his single most important lesson is verification: CI-based testing on Linux and macOS is what makes closed-loop autonomy safe, and "companies under-invest in it."
  • The practitioners agree: master a small stack, don't chase the hype. Tina Huang runs a deliberately small core, Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, Warp for building; Claude, Perplexity, and a local agent for thinking, and hosts open models (Qwen, Llama, GLM, MiniMax) on a Mac Studio for privacy and cost. Base44's Base1 model, trained on tens of millions of user interactions, is the corporate version of the same instinct: own a layer of the stack so you're not just renting someone else's frontier.

The Jobs Math Refuses to Resolve

  • The data cuts both ways, hard. A Ramp/Revelio study of 22,000 companies found that heavy AI adopters (about $30 per employee per month) grew headcount 10.2%, with entry-level roles up 12%, the opposite of the "AI eats the juniors" narrative. But it sits against ~90,000 AI-linked job cuts through May and a Goldman estimate of ~16,000 net jobs erased monthly, falling hardest on Gen Z. Firms only piloting AI saw no gains at all.
  • The agent economy is starting to transact with itself. OKX launched a marketplace where AI agents hire, pay, and rate one another on-chain, with CEO Star Xu's pitch that "every individual effectively gains an unlimited workforce." Read next to Spotify's auto-authored PRs and OKX's $25B valuation, the abstract "agents working for agents" future suddenly has rails and a price.

The Throughline

Strip the week to one sentence and it's this: the model is no longer the scarce thing, and almost nobody's behavior has caught up to that. Washington is busy rationing access to GPT-5.6 and export-controlling Mythos as though the weights were the asset, while a free Chinese model takes a top-five intelligence score and an MIT-licensed model from a lab no one has heard of draws pelicans at 103 tokens a second. You cannot vet your way out of an open frontier. The genuinely uncontrollable capability isn't the one behind the government's gate; it's the one already sitting on Hugging Face.

What's actually scarce is everything around the model, and that's the throughline connecting the week's most concrete stories. Nate Jones calls it the context war. IndyDevDan calls it the model stack. Spotify calls it verification, and backs it with the only hard numbers in the issue: 73% of PRs AI-authored only because they first built CI that could catch what the agents got wrong. Tina Huang calls it mastering a small core. These are four different vocabularies for the same realization, that the value migrated from the weights to the scaffolding, the context, and the judgment wrapped around them. The labs are still selling models. The people winning are buying systems.

That reframing is what makes the government's posture look so confused. California is right that frontier AI is becoming a utility, and it's subsidizing access accordingly. Washington is treating it as a controlled munition. The Pentagon is treating Anthropic as a security liability. All three can't be correct, and the open frontier is quietly settling the argument by making the gated version look beside the point. IndyDevDan's resilience pitch, own open weights so you can't be rug-pulled, is the buyer-side version of the same logic: when the closed frontier can be switched off by a vendor or a regulator, the open one stops being a budget choice and becomes an insurance policy.

The Bigger Picture

We are watching raw capability commoditize in real time, and the value rush up the stack to integration, reliability, and trust, the same life cycle that databases, web servers, and operating systems all went through. Each of those went from scarce magic to cheap substrate, and the money moved to whoever owned the layer of context and reliability on top. Frontier models are entering that phase faster than anyone priced in. South Korea's $518 billion bet on memory fabs and Arena's leap to $100 million in revenue for simply ranking everyone else's models are both signs of where the durable value is pooling: in the physical substrate underneath and the trust infrastructure on top, not in the model in the middle that anyone can now download a near-equivalent of.

The unresolved jobs math is the human face of the same transition. The Ramp data showing AI-heavy firms hiring more, even at entry level, isn't a contradiction of the 90,000 cuts; it's what a value migration looks like from inside. The firms that win the harness, Spotify shipping 4,500 times a day, the well-resourced "AI exploiters", grow into the capability and pull headcount up with them. The firms that bought a model and expected it to be the system stall, pilot, and shed people. Bloomberg's "terrible dilemma" is exactly this fork: the same technology that makes a Spotify more productive makes a laggard more disposable, and there's no clean policy that captures the first without absorbing the second.

And the agent economy is the leading edge of where this goes next. OKX wiring agents to hire and pay each other isn't a gimmick once you've seen Spotify's agents authoring three-quarters of its code; it's the natural extension, work flowing between models with crypto as the settlement layer and humans drifting toward the verification-and-judgment role Gustavsson keeps insisting is the one that matters. The week's quiet warning, sitting under all of it, is Tina Huang going deliberately "low AI" for the one thing she wants to feel human. As the harness gets better at everything else, the scarce resource left may be the judgment about where not to point it.

What to Watch

  • Whether other states follow California, or the Pentagon's posture wins. A half-price Claude deal for the biggest state government is a template. Watch whether other states sign similar deals, validating frontier-AI-as-utility, or whether the federal "supply-chain risk" framing spreads and freezes Anthropic out of the public sector more broadly.
  • Whether enterprises actually run open weights, or just benchmark them. GLM-5.2 and Qwen 3.6 winning on paper is one thing; a Fortune 500 self-hosting one in production, accepting IndyDevDan's $50-100K hardware reality, is another. The first disclosed open-model deployments would confirm the resilience thesis is moving from argument to practice.
  • Whether verification becomes the visible bottleneck. Spotify says companies under-invest in it and that auto-merged PRs forced them to strengthen test automation. As agent-authored code spreads, watch for verification, CI, and judgment infrastructure to become the thing leaders actually compete on, the way Spotify already is.
