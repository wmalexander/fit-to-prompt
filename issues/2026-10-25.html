AI Economy · Compute

Here’s the ‘Core Economic Flaw’ in the AI Bull Case as Stock and Bond Trades Unravel, According to a Top Economist

Fortune · Oct 24

Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok spent the past week issuing a series of warnings about the AI boom as Wall Street pulled back from the sector. On Tuesday he pointed to a recent slowdown in token spending growth, saying the demand curve for compute is flat and highly sensitive to price. Most companies investing in the technology, he added, have yet to see significant productivity returns, so AI looks more like a cost center than a growth driver.

Slok’s core argument is that the marginal cost of producing compute exceeds the marginal benefit it delivers to users, which raises questions about claims of unlimited demand for data centers. He argues the main determinant in data-center construction models is not physical capacity or power but the price users are willing to pay, and that companies will only pay a high price if it brings higher revenue growth, bigger profits, or substantial savings. “Maybe that will come in the future. Maybe not,” he warned. On Thursday he also flagged “circularity” in token spending, saying 80% of the top 1% of AI spenders are VC-backed.

AI Research · Coding

GVS5H: Five Qwen3.8-27B Models Match Claude Fable 5 on LiveCodeBench Hard

GitHub · Sep 23

A training-free method has fresh instances of one model split a problem and coordinate through a shared filesystem holding a plan, notes, and the current solution. On the 100 latest hard LiveCodeBench problems, orchestrated Qwen3.8 Flash Next reached 93.0% pass@1 against Fable 5’s 90.4% at 9% of the cost, and locally served Qwen3.8-27B rose from 66.8% to 92.4%. The authors note gains are not universal, and some models were unchanged or worse.

AI Infrastructure · Chips

How Cerebras Deploys Trillion-Parameter AI Models

Cerebras · Oct 22

Using the roughly 3-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 as its example, Cerebras explains that a single WSE-3T wafer holds 44 GB of on-chip memory. At 4-bit weights each of the model’s 93 layers needs about 16 GB, which puts one dense layer or two linear-attention layers on each wafer and totals 59 wafers. Only activations cross between wafers, at about 2 microseconds per hop, for 1,000 to 2,000+ tokens per second.

The Throughline

Several of today’s sources are, without knowing it, arguing about the same variable: price per unit of useful work. Slok says demand for compute is price-sensitive and the payoff is slow. GVS5H shows small open models reaching Fable 5-class scores on a coding benchmark at 9% of the cost. Berman says cost per completed task is the fair comparison for Kimi. Even Hamaili’s supply-chain thesis and Conrad’s credit thesis in the AI:AM episode are about who pays for capacity. If intelligence keeps getting cheaper per task, the data-center bull case depends on demand growing faster than prices fall, and Slok is saying the evidence for that is thin.

The second thread is trust, and it runs on the same cheapness. A three-second clip yields an 85% match voice, Block ships a speech model that runs locally, and Jones warns that AI keys are now worth stealing like cash. The election study adds a quieter version of the problem: when you force a model to choose, it chooses, and the choice differs sharply between vendors. OpenAI’s response, that the consumer app refuses while the API complies, is a reminder that safety behavior often lives in the product wrapper, not the model. That is a fragile place for it to live when the model underneath is cheap and widely available.

The third thread is absorption. TechCrunch’s graveyard keeps returning to platforms folding a startup’s feature into their own product, from Relay to Atlas. Text-message agents are the next crowded category, and the entry is a $1-a-day price. At the labor end, Politano’s creative-jobs data and Korchevskyi’s teacher argument ask the same question about people instead of products: when the machine does the middle of the work, what is left that anyone will pay a human for?

The Bigger Picture

The AI story is shifting from capability to economics. For three years the question was what models can do, and today’s sources keep asking what it costs and who is paid. Slok’s warning, small models matching larger ones through orchestration, and an inference stack that spreads a 3-trillion-parameter model across 59 wafers all point to the same tension. The cost of delivering an answer is falling, and the cost of building to deliver it is still rising.

My read is that both can be true for a while, which is exactly what makes a boom fragile. Capacity gets built on the assumption of unlimited demand, and efficiency gains quietly cut that demand. The social side runs the same way: cheap voice cloning and cheap tutors arrive before the norms, such as safe words and human teachers, that make them safe. The practical advice for anyone building with these tools is unglamorous. Track cost per completed task, assume your voice and keys are exposed, and expect your product to be a feature on someone else’s platform sooner than you think.

What to Watch

  • Token spending data. Slok says growth has slowed. More surveys or earnings commentary confirming it would strengthen his case, and a rebound would undercut it.
  • Replication of GVS5H. The code and transcripts are public, so independent runs on other benchmarks will show whether the gains hold beyond hard LiveCodeBench problems.
  • Kimi K3.1 Pro’s open weights. The paid-influencer allegations are unresolved, so independent evaluations will matter more than launch-week buzz.
  • Voice safeguards. With local, human-sounding speech models arriving, watch for banks and carriers moving to verification that does not rely on how someone sounds.
