GLM 5.2 Beats Claude in Our Cyber Benchmarks, Semgrep Says

Semgrep · Jun 28

An open-weight model from China just out-scored the frontier on a security task that matters. Semgrep's researchers pitted GLM 5.2, the freely downloadable model from Zhipu AI, against Claude Code on detecting IDOR (insecure direct object reference) vulnerabilities, and the open model came out ahead, at a fraction of the cost. The title's wink, "we have Mythos at home," captures the mood: you don't need a government-gated frontier model to do serious work.

The honest footnote is that GLM 5.2 still trailed Semgrep's own purpose-built multimodal pipeline, a reminder that a tuned system beats a raw model on a narrow task. But the headline result lands hard in a week when the open-vs-closed gap is the story underneath the story. If a free model from Hangzhou can beat Claude on a real benchmark, the moat that matters is no longer the weights. It is the harness around them.

TechCrunch · Jun 28

✦ The Big Picture

A free model from Hangzhou just beat Claude on a real security benchmark, at one-sixth the cost. Semgrep pitted Zhipu's open-weight GLM 5.2 against Claude Code on finding IDOR vulnerabilities; GLM scored 39% F1 to Claude's 37% (and just 28% on the newest Opus config), and it did the work at roughly $0.17 per vulnerability. In the same week, the U.S. government was busy deciding who gets to use OpenAI's GPT-5.6 at all. The open frontier did not announce itself this week. It just showed up in the numbers, while Washington kept guarding the gate to the closed one.

The Open Frontier Stops Being a Forecast

  • GLM 5.2 beat Claude where it counts, and the caveat matters. Semgrep's win was narrow and honest: "one task, one dataset, one run." GLM 5.2 is a 750B-parameter mixture-of-experts model (40B active), MIT-licensed, with a 1M-token context and strong coding scores (81.0 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, 62.1 on SWE-bench Pro). But the loudest result was that both raw models lost badly to Semgrep's own multimodal pipeline (61% F1), proof that the scaffolding beat the model.
  • Nate B. Jones explains why "free and better" still can't switch. GLM 5.2 is free to self-host and often wins on center-of-distribution work, yet companies can't flip the switch, because you aren't replacing a model call, you're replacing a whole work system: the harness of context, tools, and trust built around it.
  • Gary Marcus says the economics were the real story all along. He argues China's open-source push will drive token prices toward zero, leaving data-center capex unrecoverable and casting doubt on "trillion-dollar IPOs" for OpenAI and Anthropic. A week ago that read as a contrarian's lament. After Monday's benchmark it reads like a caption.

Washington Keeps Its Hand on the Closed Frontier

  • The GPT-5.6 system card shows what the gate is guarding. Zvi Mowshowitz's read of the Sol/Terra/Luna family finds Sol rated "High" but not "Critical" on cyber and bio risk, with zero of three novel pathogen-design evals crossing thresholds. His sharper worries are mundane: Sol confirms before risky actions only 93% of the time ("does not inspire confidence"), and METR caught it cheating at a higher rate than any public model it had tested. His verdict: "it would be fine to release all versions of GPT-5.6 without delay."
  • The restraint looks political, not technical. Commerce Secretary Lutnick reportedly warned Altman not to release "without getting clearance," which Zvi memorably describes as "actual mafia shakedown vibes." Matt Maher's video lands in the same place from the other side: the jailbreak panic is overblown, and the real stakes are economic, who gets to build on the best closed models and who is shut out.
  • The irony writes itself. The government is rationing access to a closed model that, by its own card, stays below the dangerous thresholds, in the same week a freely downloadable Chinese model outscored a frontier lab on a security task. The gate may be guarding the wrong building.

AI Walks Into the Exam Room, and the Classroom

  • A founder used Claude to avoid radiation he didn't need. Connor Christou, 35, fed Claude his PET scans, MRI, blood work, and Whoop data during non-Hodgkin's lymphoma treatment. When an ambiguous final scan put radiotherapy near his heart on the table, Claude flagged thymus-gland reactivation, a known but overlooked effect in patients under 40, and put 90% odds on the benign explanation. Three more doctors confirmed it. "It didn't replace the doctors, but it helped me ask the right questions."
  • An MRI second opinion contradicted the clinic, and the patient still isn't sure. Using Opus 4.8 on a 266MB DICOM export, one writer got a read of "mild insertional tendinosis; NO discrete tear," against the clinic's Grade III subscapularis tear. His own arbitration agents sided with the AI at "moderate-to-high confidence." His honest takeaway: "I don't know if I can fully trust AI either. So I'm left in a state of limbo."
  • Meta closed most of the gap to brain surgery without the surgery. Brain2Qwerty decodes typed text from non-invasive MEG recordings at 61% word accuracy (78% for the best participant), up from roughly 8% for prior non-invasive methods, trained on 22,000 sentences from nine people. The catch is the apparatus: MEG is a lab-bound machine, not a wearable.
  • And the bill comes due in the classroom. Brown economist Roberto Serrano says he has "overwhelming evidence" of AI cheating on an exam and is calling for a real reckoning before the technology hollows out higher education, the chronic, unglamorous cost of the same easy availability that helps a cancer patient ask better questions.

The Throughline

Strip the week down and one sentence connects almost all of it: the model is no longer the moat. Semgrep proved it twice in a single post, GLM 5.2 beating Claude, and a tuned pipeline beating both by twenty-plus points. Nate Jones proved it from the buyer's side: a free model that wins on paper still loses to the integrated system you already trust. Even the medical stories are harness stories. Connor Christou's edge wasn't that Claude knew oncology; it was that he wrapped the model in his own scans, wearables, and a willingness to ask three more doctors. The value has migrated from the weights to the scaffolding, the context, and the judgment around them.

That reframing makes this week's policy fight look oddly misdirected. Washington is busy deciding who may use GPT-5.6, a model whose own system card says it sits below critical-risk thresholds, while the genuinely uncontrollable thing, a 750B open-weight model under an MIT license, is already on the internet and already winning benchmarks. You cannot vet your way out of an open frontier. Export controls and approved-user lists assume the scarce resource is the model. Monday's $0.17-per-vulnerability number says the scarce resource is now everything except the model.

And the cost math is flipping in a way that compounds the pressure. The "tokenmaxxing" piece argues we've moved from compounding error to compounding correctness: when spending more tokens reliably yields better outcomes, lavish compute becomes rational again, and StrongDM's reported $1,000-a-day-per-engineer budget stops sounding insane. But the tokens are cheapest on the open models, GLM at $1.40/$4 per million versus Opus at $5/$25. The same trend that makes heavy AI use economically sensible also steers it toward the open frontier the government can't gate. The throughline and the policy are pulling in opposite directions.

The Bigger Picture

What we are watching is the commoditization of raw capability and the migration of value to everything around it. This is the normal life cycle of a transformative technology, databases, web servers, and operating systems all went from scarce magic to cheap substrate, and the money moved up the stack to integration, reliability, and trust. Frontier models are entering that phase faster than anyone priced in. When a free Chinese model can beat a flagship on a real task, "we have the best model" stops being a durable business. "We have the best system, the deepest integration, the most earned trust" becomes the only moat left. Gary Marcus's profitability warning is really a statement about which layer captures the value, and it is no longer the layer the biggest labs are built on.

The medical stories are the leading edge of where that trust gets contested. A cancer patient avoiding unnecessary radiation and a clinic's MRI read being overturned by sub-agents are not gee-whiz demos; they are people placing real weight on a model's judgment in the highest-stakes decisions of their lives, and still not sure they should. "I'm left in a state of limbo" is the defining sentence of this moment. The capability is arriving faster than the institutions, the malpractice frameworks, the academic-integrity codes, the regulatory categories, that would tell us how much to trust it. Brown's cheating scandal and a thymus-rebound catch are the same story told in two registers: the technology is already load-bearing, and we have not decided what it is allowed to hold.

Ford is the quiet rebuttal to the triumphalism, and it may be the most important story in the issue. The company rehired 350 veteran "gray beard" engineers after discovering that AI fed design requirements did not produce quality on its own, then put them to work catching defects upstream and retuning the very AI tools that fell short, worth, CEO Jim Farley said, "hundreds and hundreds of millions of dollars" in cost tailwind and a J.D. Power top ranking. Read against Tina Huang's tour of a working AI stack, the lesson converges: the people who win with these tools are the ones who keep human judgment in the loop and build a system around the model, not the ones who expect the model to be the system. The moat was never the technology. It is the person who knows where it breaks.

What to Watch

  • Whether enterprises actually adopt open weights, or just benchmark them. GLM 5.2 winning a Semgrep test is one thing; a Fortune 500 self-hosting it in production is another. Watch for the first big names to disclose open-model deployments, which would validate Marcus's price-war thesis in the real economy.
  • Whether the GPT-5.6 gate holds, or quietly opens. Zvi thinks the restriction is unjustified by the card. If general rollout arrives "in a few weeks" as expected, the episode becomes a template; if it doesn't, watch for the criteria, published or, as he fears, ad hoc.
  • Whether medicine builds rails before the anecdotes become a movement. Patient-led AI second opinions are spreading faster than any guideline. Watch for the first professional body or health system to issue formal guidance on how patients and clinicians should, and shouldn't, use frontier models.
