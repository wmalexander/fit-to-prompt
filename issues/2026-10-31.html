Cracks Are Emerging in the AI Spending Boom

TechCrunch · Oct 30

Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft have outlined plans to spend more than $740 billion on capital expenditures this year, and investors who once rewarded such outlays are now worried about negative free cash flow, mounting debt, and whether the returns will ever arrive. The Nasdaq fell 2.9% on Friday. Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion for the third quarter, its second straight quarter in the red, after a 12% drop on Thursday that wiped out $500 billion in market value.

Amazon reported negative free cash flow of $42.4 billion over the past 12 months and expects $220 billion in capital spending this year, and Meta is guiding to as much as $145 billion. Microsoft was the exception, with $18.6 billion in positive free cash flow. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle raised a combined $326 billion in debt in 2026, triple last year, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence. TechCrunch notes the muddied waters could complicate Anthropic’s IPO.

✦ The Big Picture

Amazon’s free cash flow over the past 12 months was negative $42.4 billion, against positive $14.8 billion the year before. That swing is the sharpest single number in today’s news: the biggest AI spenders are now funding the buildout with debt and stock, and the market has started to punish them for it. The thread running through the rest of the issue is who is willing to sign for the bill.

Today’s Headlines

Paying for the Buildout

  • Cracks in the boom – Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft have outlined more than $740 billion in capital spending this year. The Nasdaq fell 2.9% on Friday, with Nvidia down 4%, Broadcom more than 5%, and Qualcomm 11%. Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion for the quarter while capex grew to $51.4 billion. Microsoft was the exception, with $18.6 billion in positive free cash flow, and it blamed third-party capacity delays rather than a pullback. Five of the largest buyers raised a combined $326 billion in debt this year, triple last year’s total.
  • Anthropic’s credit rating – The Financial Times reports that Blackstone, the anchor investor in a $63 billion deal to finance Anthropic’s chips, now expects a double-B rating after the IPO, the top tier of speculative grade. Blackstone had previously said investment grade was likely before 2027. It is only a forecast, but a junk rating would make the borrowing that funds Anthropic’s compute commitments costlier.
  • Nvidia as cosigner – The Information reported, and Bloomberg corroborated, that Nvidia is in talks to guarantee billions in debt for Nscale’s planned Anthropic data center in West Virginia. Anthropic has signed a $44.6 billion contract for the first 460-megawatt building, and a second deal worth roughly $45 billion is under discussion. Nvidia’s earlier support covered leases and chip purchases, not construction, so this would be a step further out on the risk curve.
  • Google admits AI Mode costs it – Latent Space quotes Google’s chief business officer saying that strong growth in AI Mode queries had an impact because of lower monetization there. The newsletter’s headline puts the stock drop at 15%, while TechCrunch counts 12% on Thursday before a further fall Friday, so treat the exact figure as dependent on the day you measure.
  • Factories for data centers – Runware wants to build pods on a production line, scaling a Romania-based factory to 30 megawatts of capacity per day by 2028, nearly 1 gigawatt a month. That would roughly match the 10 gigawatts the entire U.S. was expected to add this year. Google’s Suncatcher satellite is the other end of the same impulse: four TPUs and 1 kW of power in orbit, able to run one chip for about 20 minutes at a time.

Agents Meet the Real World

  • DoorDash’s reality check – The company said it has received only “several hundred” orders from third-party agents, against roughly 3 million deliveries a day. CEO Tony Xu said “you never want to fight gravity” but also that you do not want to be delusional. Amazon has been blocking personal agents while Walmart has welcomed them, and DoorDash sees a possible future in advertising inside agents.
  • Zest and Ollie – Zest turned its restaurant app into a text-message concierge that learns tastes from food and drink card transactions imported through Plaid. Ollie, a family assistant that was among the first mainstream apps to reach SOC 2 and had raised a $7.5 million seed led by Khosla Ventures, will shut down November 6. TechCrunch’s own aside is that privacy has never been a hugely profitable part of tech.
  • An AI-native engineering org – In a thread, Sashank Gondala of FurtherAI says the number of code changes each engineer merges per day has grown about 5x since January while the revert rate stayed under half a percent. He says coding agents alone did not get them there; the team reworked each step of shipping, and a designer has merged about 50 changes since August.
  • Testing Copilot without real data – Microsoft Research describes full-scale synthetic enterprise environments, grounded in public data, used to evaluate Copilot where real customer data cannot be touched, and calls the result the first standardized evaluation baseline for the product.

Security and Research

  • A backdoored Claude Code extension – Phorion found that versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 of the Claude Code for VS Code extension on the Cursor Marketplace carried a persistent SSH backdoor, and Cursor has removed them. On macOS the malware’s attempt to enable SSH fails when run as a standard user, which limits the impact there, but it still installs persistence and sends host reconnaissance to the operator. The advisory’s update says Cursor’s notice covers both versions on macOS and Windows.
  • A sleep phase for models – Google Research proposes giving LLMs a periodic sleep phase that consolidates short-term context into long-term parameters, aimed at the problem that deployed models are frozen at a knowledge cutoff and that continual updates cause catastrophic forgetting.
  • Mapping forest types – DeepMind and Google Research, with WRI and IIASA, released a 10-meter global map that separates natural forests, plantations, and farms, plus a 2017 to 2025 time series. It is meant as a baseline for the EU’s deforestation regulation.
  • Deskilling and tinkering – SigNoz asks whether AI handling 95% of incident response leaves SREs worse at the 5% that matters, citing Bainbridge’s 1983 “Ironies of Automation.” On the lighter side, Drew Breunig baked a 6502 CPU emulator into the weights of Qwen3.5-4B so it can run NES ROMs, very slowly, and NVIDIA published a Nemotron-powered haunted house project.

Skeptics and Analysts

  • Theranos’ whistleblower – Tyler Shultz says the AI boom is “a great opportunity to go deceive people.” TechCrunch frames the warning around companies that have never turned a profit raising capital at trillion-dollar valuations.
  • Stratechery and the WSJ – Ben Thompson’s weekly roundup lists Anthropic numbers and the end of the Internet as we know it, and its lead piece argues that Stripe does not make any business succeed but makes more things practical to try. The Wall Street Journal headline says OpenAI raised less than half of its $30 billion target in the latest round; the article was paywalled, so only the headline is cited.

The Throughline

Read the first stories together and one idea comes through. The AI buildout used to be financed by profits and is now financed by promises. Alphabet’s free cash flow is negative while its capex climbs to $51.4 billion a quarter. Nvidia is considering cosigning a customer’s construction loans. Blackstone is walking back its investment-grade forecast for a company whose data center contract alone is $44.6 billion. Each of these is a way of moving risk onto someone else’s balance sheet, and each new guarantor makes the next round of risk easier to hide.

The demand side is more modest than the spending implies. DoorDash has seen several hundred agent orders in total against about 3 million deliveries a day, Ollie is shutting down, and Google concedes that its own AI answers earn less per query than the old results. TechCrunch notes that cloud demand itself is strong, so the doubt is about returns, not usage. Gondala’s thread is the counterpoint: inside one engineering team, agents did change throughput, but only after the team rebuilt the process around them.

The Claude Code extension compromise is a reminder that the tools themselves are now supply-chain targets. An extension runs with the permissions of the editor, so a backdoored bundle reaches source code and credentials immediately.

The Bigger Picture

The question hanging over the industry is who holds the risk when the returns arrive late. In 2026 the answer is shifting from shareholders to lenders and suppliers: a debt raise triple last year’s, a chipmaker offering guarantees, and credit agencies about to grade the labs. That is how infrastructure cycles usually mature, and it tends to reward whoever has cash and punish whoever has ambition alone.

At the same time, the products are meeting ordinary friction. Agents are a rounding error in a delivery company’s order book, privacy-first assistants cannot pay for themselves, and experts like Shultz are drawing comparisons to past frauds. None of this stops the buildout, but it shortens the patience of the people paying for it. If the next quarter’s numbers look like this one, expect the story to move from how capable the models are to how long the spending can last.

What to Watch

  • Anthropic’s IPO and rating. The company filed to go public this week. Watch whether the double-B forecast holds and how it changes the terms of the $63 billion chip financing.
  • More guarantees. If Nvidia cosigns Nscale’s loans, expect other suppliers to be asked for the same. Watch whether investors treat that as support or as a warning.
  • Extension security. Check whether you installed Claude Code for VS Code 2.1.312 or 2.1.313 through Cursor, and watch for further notices from Cursor and Anthropic.
