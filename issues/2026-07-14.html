Legal · Apple v. OpenAI

The Wildest Allegations in Apple's Trade Secrets Lawsuit Against OpenAI

TechCrunch · Jul 13

Apple's 41-page complaint reads less like a filing than a screenplay. It names Chang Liu, a former Apple senior systems electrical engineer now at OpenAI, and alleges he exploited authentication bugs to reach systems he had no business reaching. The complaint quotes him directly: "LOL, I found out I can access the [network storage], so funny." Elsewhere: "Didn't even know we could take those from the office." Apple alleges OpenAI directed job candidates to bring Apple parts and prototypes to interviews, coached departing employees to evade exit procedures, and advised them not to sign exit agreements. The filing calls OpenAI "rotten to its core" and warns this is "the tip of the iceberg."

The context is what gives the suit its weight and also its air of grievance. More than 400 former Apple employees now work at OpenAI. OpenAI's chief hardware officer, Tang Yew Tan, spent 24 years at Apple, most recently as VP of product design for iPhone and Apple Watch. OpenAI bought Jony Ive's io for $6.5 billion last year. Apple says it first raised concerns in February. OpenAI's public response was four sentences long: "We have no interest in other companies' trade secrets." Ben Thompson's read, in a companion piece, is that there is one genuinely guilty employee here and that the rest "mostly feels like lashing out."

Infrastructure · Energy

Data Centers Have Already Hiked Electricity Prices on the Public by $23 Billion

Fortune · Jul 14

The number is not a projection about some future grid crunch. It is money already moving: $23 billion in customer rate increases running through 2028, driven by data center demand. The distribution is the story. Large data centers use flexible consumption arrangements to minimize the share of costs allocated to them, while residential consumers absorb what is left. Clawing it back, as the headline concedes, is not really a thing the rate-setting process is built to do.

✦ The Big Picture

Data centers have already added $23 billion to other people's electricity bills, and the mechanism is almost elegant: because data centers "often are able to fine-tune their electricity consumption, using more one minute and less another," they can go quiet during the exact hours the meter that sets their rate is watching. Everyone who cannot time-shift their life around a coincident-peak measurement window pays the difference. In June, a Utah State Senate President lost his seat over a data center. Today's issue is the sound of the AI boom's bill arriving at addresses that never ordered anything.

Today's Headlines

The Bill Arrives

  • The $23 billion is not a forecast. It is already in the rates. Theodore Kury of the University of Florida traces it through PJM, the market covering 14 mid-Atlantic and Midwest states, with the increases running until at least the end of 2028. Two mechanisms do the work. Coincident-peak pricing lets data centers minimize consumption during the measured hours that set their bills. And stranded-asset risk means that if a project underperforms or is cancelled, "any costs the utility company incurred will be spread among all the other customers." Many tech companies have pledged to pay their fair share. Kury's point is that the pledge does not survive contact with how tariffs actually work.
  • Voters have started removing people over this. Utah State Senate President Stuart Adams was unseated in June after backing a major data center project. In Michigan, 30-plus data centers have been proposed in two years, and Google's Van Buren Township facility alone would draw 2.7 gigawatts, about what two million homes use. Gallup found in March that 70 percent of Americans oppose AI data centers in their own communities. Maine passed a moratorium in April. The jobs argument, notes researcher Lauren Mullenbach, is the weak link: the facilities create very few permanent jobs relative to the tax incentives handed over.
  • Meta's Hyperion is the whole argument compressed into one parish. Richland Parish, Louisiana, population roughly 20,000, is hosting a project that went from $10 billion to more than $50 billion in under two years, and from 2 gigawatts to 5. The upside is real and specific: $1.6 billion contracted with local companies, bus drivers earning over $80,000 against a regional median income of $42,000, teacher bonuses up to $50,000 in a state where the average teacher salary is $56,785. So is the downside. "There is nowhere to go if you can't pay triple prices," says 34-year-old resident Erika James. Entergy is building ten new power plants. Louisiana exempted data centers from state sales tax for 20 years.

The Throughline

Read the energy stories and the economics story together and a single structure emerges: the costs of the AI boom are being routed to people who have no seat at the table where the routing happens. Kury's analysis is the cleanest statement of it, because the mechanism is not corruption, it is arithmetic. Rates are set by measuring demand during coincident peak hours. Data centers can dial consumption down during those hours. A retired couple in Ohio cannot. The $23 billion is not a scandal in the sense of anyone breaking a rule; it is what happens when a tariff structure written for factories meets a customer that can see the meter coming.

Now notice that Brynjolfsson's finding has exactly the same shape. The aggregate labor market looks stable, which is the number that gets reported and the number that reassures. Underneath it, 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed roles are losing jobs at more than 4 percent a year. Same pattern: the headline metric is fine because the harm is concentrated in a group that does not move the headline metric. Residential ratepayers are under-represented in rate proceedings. Twenty-three-year-olds are under-represented in aggregate employment statistics. In both cases the system is technically working and specific people are technically absorbing it, and the gap between those two sentences is where 2026 actually lives.

Which is why the economists' statement is more interesting than its own headline. Two hundred economists, sixteen Nobel laureates, and the chief economists of the two leading labs signed a document whose central claim is we cannot see this. And Slok's finding is the knife: there are five competing definitions of "AI exposure," and they rank occupations differently. Every confident claim about AI and jobs, in either direction, is downstream of a definitional choice nobody has settled. Meanwhile Balazs says the entire discipline of marketing has lost its instrumentation because customers now ask a model instead of a search engine. Nadella tells enterprises they cannot see the value they are giving away in their own corrections. The through-line is not "AI is bad." It is that we have built something enormously consequential and simultaneously blinded most of the instruments we would use to steer it.

And then, at the top of the page, Apple and OpenAI are suing each other over prototypes, and Musk and Altman are calling each other scammers on the internet while both companies queue for the public markets. It is genuinely funny to put those two halves of the issue side by side. The people best positioned to know what this technology is doing to electricity markets and entry-level employment spent the week fighting about who took whose metal-finishing techniques. Thompson's phrase for the lawsuit, "lashing out," generalizes further than he meant it to. The industry is producing an enormous amount of heat about ownership and priority and credit, at precisely the moment when the questions that will actually determine how this goes are boring, technical, and unresolved: who pays for the transformer, who hires the 23-year-old, and what "exposure" even means.

The Bigger Picture

For three years the binding constraint on AI was capability, and every argument about the technology was really an argument about whether the models would keep getting better. That question is now boring, because the answer is obviously yes, and today's issue is what the next constraint looks like: social license. Seventy percent of Americans oppose an AI data center in their own community. Maine has a moratorium. A state Senate President is out of a job. This is no longer a discourse problem the industry can solve with a better blog post, because it has crossed into the one domain where the industry's usual tools do not work at all, which is local electoral politics conducted by people whose power bill went up and who do not care what a scaling law is.

What makes this genuinely dangerous for the buildout is that the benefits and costs have different shapes. The Louisiana story is the honest version, and I would resist reading it as a simple indictment: bus drivers making $80,000 in a parish with a $42,000 median income is a real thing that really happened to real families, and so is a $50,000 teacher bonus. But the piece is careful to note that most of that upside is tied to the construction phase, which ends, while the 5 gigawatts of draw, the ten new power plants, and the tripled rents do not. A boom that pays wages for three years and raises the cost of living permanently is a bad trade dressed as a good one, and the residents figuring that out are the same people who vote in June primaries.

The deeper shift is a transfer of who gets to define reality. The economists have conceded they cannot measure the thing. The marketers have conceded they cannot measure the thing. The utility commissions are allocating billions using cost-causation rules that a sufficiently clever load can arbitrage. Into that vacuum steps a very confident industry narrative, and Nadella's warning is quietly the most subversive item in the issue, because he is a vendor telling customers that the standard arrangement extracts more from them than they realize. When the CEO of OpenAI's largest partner starts advocating for swappable orchestration layers and the open-weight share of one gateway's traffic hits 29 percent, that is not idealism, it is a bet on where power is going. And it rhymes with the Louisiana parish and the 23-year-old and the ratepayer: the story of the next two years is not whether the models get smarter. It is whether the people absorbing the costs get organized before the arrangements harden.

What to Watch

  • Whether any state utility commission rewrites coincident-peak tariffs. Kury identified the exact mechanism moving the $23 billion, and it is a rule, which means it is changeable. Watch PJM states specifically. If one commission changes how large flexible loads are measured, it becomes the template, and the entire economics of colocating a data center changes with it.
  • Whether Stuart Adams turns out to be a data point or a trend. One unseated state Senate President is an anecdote. The November elections, against a backdrop of 70 percent local opposition and a Maine moratorium already on the books, are the test. If more incumbents lose over data centers, the siting math changes faster than any federal policy could change it.
  • Whether enterprises actually act on Nadella's warning. The open-weight share of Vercel gateway traffic (29 percent) is the metric to track, because it is the only one that measures whether "we should own our learning loop" is a belief or a behavior. Talk is free. Migrating off a frontier API is not.
  • Whether anyone converges on a definition of "AI exposure." Five competing frameworks producing different occupational rankings means every policy argument currently being made about AI and jobs is resting on a choice its author probably did not disclose. Until that settles, treat confident labor-market claims, including optimistic ones, as unfalsifiable.
