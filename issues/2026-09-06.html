The Pelican Comparison Grid for Astra Is Pretty Interesting

Simon Willison's Weblog · Sep 4

Simon Willison ran his standing "draw an SVG pelican riding a bicycle" test against GPT-6 Astra across multiple reasoning-effort levels, then laid the results out in a comparison grid alongside the GPT-5.6 family of models it replaces.

The grid shows Astra producing noticeably more coherent pelican illustrations than its predecessors, and doing it while burning through fewer tokens per generation — a small but consistent benchmark that Willison has used across several model launches now, and one that continues to track reasonably well with broader capability gains.

✦ The Big Picture

Simon Willison's cheapest reasoning tier of a brand-new model called Astra beat every reasoning tier of its predecessor "Sol" on his standing pelican-drawing test — for 9.55 cents. That's not a headline you'd get from a benchmark leaderboard. It's the kind of signal you only notice if you run the same quirky test on every model release and actually look at what changed.

Today's Headlines

AI Models

  • The Pelican Comparison Grid for Astra Is Pretty Interesting — Willison ran his "SVG pelican riding a bicycle" test against a new model he calls GPT-6 Astra at five reasoning levels (low through max) and put the results in a grid next to the GPT-5.6 family it replaces (Sol, Terra, Luna). His verdict: "The Astra pelicans are much better," while even the best Sol output is "still pretty clearly a bunch of abstract shapes." Astra costs more per token — $10/$50 per million input/output tokens versus Sol's $5/$30 — but uses far fewer tokens per generation, and a token-count quirk (Astra and Luna both used exactly 16 input tokens for the same prompt, versus 26 for Sol and Terra) has Willison speculating Astra and Luna share more architecture than the vendor has disclosed.

The Throughline

The interesting thing about Willison's test isn't the pelicans themselves — it's that a silly, consistent, self-administered benchmark keeps surfacing real signal about how a model family is built. The 16-versus-26 input token gap between Astra/Luna and Sol/Terra isn't something a vendor's own marketing page would ever tell you. It's the kind of detail that only shows up when someone runs the exact same prompt across a whole model lineup and diffs the receipts.

That's also why the price framing matters more than it looks. A naive read of "$10/$50 versus $5/$30" says Astra is twice as expensive. But token efficiency flips that: if Astra needs meaningfully fewer output tokens to hit a better result, the effective cost per useful output can land below the cheaper-looking model. Willison's own math — the best Astra tier beating every Sol tier for under a dime — is the kind of total-cost-of-quality number that a per-token price sheet will never surface on its own.

The Bigger Picture

Zoom out and this is really a story about how the industry is quietly re-learning to distrust vendor-supplied benchmarks. As frontier labs ship faster and reasoning-effort knobs multiply (low, medium, high, xhigh, max, and whatever comes next), the practical question shifts from "which model is smartest" to "which model gives me the best result per dollar at the setting I'll actually use." Independent, repeatable, slightly absurd personal benchmarks like Willison's pelican test are filling a gap that official model cards don't: they're cheap to run, easy to compare across releases, and hard for a vendor to game after the fact.

The undisclosed-relationship speculation is worth sitting with too. As model families multiply within a single vendor's lineup (Sol, Terra, Luna, Astra), buyers increasingly can't tell from naming alone which models share weights, training data, or architecture. That opacity has real cost implications: if Astra and Luna really are closer cousins than officially stated, then routing decisions based on presumed independence between models could be quietly wrong. Expect more of this kind of forensic benchmarking — not because anyone especially cares about pelicans, but because it's becoming one of the few ways outsiders can reverse-engineer how these model families actually relate to each other.

What to Watch

  • Token efficiency as a hidden cost lever. As reasoning-effort tiers proliferate, the sticker price per million tokens matters less than tokens-consumed-per-task. Watch for more "it costs more per token but less per answer" framing from other model releases.
  • Model-family lineage claims getting tested. If Astra and Luna really do share architecture despite being marketed as distinct GPT-5.6/GPT-6 tiers, expect more independent probing (token counts, latency fingerprints, refusal patterns) to surface which "different" models are actually related.
