Popular AI Leaderboard Arena Nearly Doubles Its Valuation to $3.1B in 10 Months, and Now Ranks Models on Lying

TechCrunch · Oct 8

Arena, the crowdsourced model-ranking platform that began in 2023 as a UC Berkeley research project, has raised a $200 million Series B at a $3.1 billion valuation, led by Lightspeed and Khosla Ventures. Its January Series A was $150 million at $1.7 billion, and the company says its annualized revenue grew from $30 million then to $100 million in June.

Arena says AI is advancing faster than anyone’s ability to evaluate it, and that static benchmarks break down once models recognize they are being tested. It has added an alignment category to its leaderboard that scores unauthorized action, false attribution and what it calls deceptive completion, meaning lying about finishing a task. A slate of OpenAI models leads the preliminary rankings, with Claude Opus 5.5 in sixth place and Claude Fable in ninth.

Danu Robotics’ Fight to Build a Better Recycling Robot

TechCrunch · Oct 9

After six years of work, Edinburgh-based Danu has raised $5 million in late-seed funding for H.E.R.O., a recycling sorter that uses a pincer claw instead of suction and uses AI to keep improving its software. It has $500,000 in signed contracts and more than 200 customers in its sales pipeline. Founder Amy Ma estimates a working site would earn $485,000 in added revenue against a $160,000 up-front cost.

The Big Picture · Oct 12, 2026
Who Grades the Graders

Arena’s $200 million round values the company at $3.1 billion, up from $1.7 billion in January, and its revenue run rate more than tripled in that stretch, from $30 million to $100 million. The reason, in the company’s own words, is that “static benchmarks break down once models recognize they’re being tested.” Today’s stories are about measurement: who gets to score AI, what the score is worth, and what it takes to prove a machine works outside the demo.

Measuring the Models

  • Arena is selling the answer to benchmark gaming. The platform started in 2023 as a UC Berkeley project that crowdsourced rankings, and people still use it free by entering prompts or requesting vibe-coded projects and voting on which model did better. It claims tens of millions of monthly visitors. The money comes from AI Evaluations, a product launched last September that gives model labs and enterprises detailed performance analytics built on that community feedback. TechCrunch’s explanation for the timing is that labs found their models gaming benchmark tests this year, while companies wanted to know which model works best for their own work rather than for a standard exam.
  • The new alignment leaderboard ranks behavior, not skill. It scores unauthorized action (doing things it was not asked to do), false attribution (crediting a statement or fact to the wrong source), and deceptive completion (saying a task was done when it was not). In the preliminary rankings a slate of OpenAI models sits at the top, Claude Opus 5.5 is sixth and Claude Fable is ninth. The word that matters is preliminary, since the story does not describe how the scores are computed or how many models were compared.
  • The investor list is a map of who wants a neutral scorekeeper. Lightspeed and Khosla led, with Salesforce Ventures, Dell Technologies Capital, a16z, Felicis, 01 Advisors and Endeavor Catalyst also in. Arena frames itself as the neutral third party that should measure how safe and aligned AI is “once it’s in the hands of real people.”

Proof in the Physical World

  • Danu’s pitch is built on a number a customer can check. Founder Amy Ma got the idea as a software developer at a London bank, noticing that its separate recycling bins were all emptied into the same trash. Her Edinburgh company’s robot, H.E.R.O., sorts recycling with a pincer claw rather than the suction arms sold by competitors such as Glacier and RecycleEye. Ma says a working site would earn $485,000 in added revenue, against a $160,000 initial investment and $24,000 a year in maintenance, because recycling plants earn money from what they pull off the line. Those are the founder’s estimates, not audited results.
  • The traction is early but concrete. Danu has $500,000 in signed contracts, letters of interest from two large customers and more than 200 customers in the pipeline, against a $20 billion market for recycling sorting in Europe and North America. It raised $5 million in late-seed funding and plans to make the robot smaller and sturdier so it can sit at airports, hospitals and shopping centers. Its real edge, Ma argues, is business approach: it uses AI to continuously improve the robot’s software.
  • TechCrunch Disrupt is the next stage for both stories. Danu will take part in Startup Battlefield 200 at the event, October 13 to 15 at Moscone Center. A separate TechCrunch Events post, which is promotion rather than reporting, previews a Smart Systems Stage session with Ben Longmier of Ambrosia Energy and Bill Thayer of Bloom Energy on where the AI infrastructure boom is creating winners. The post lists the open questions as what happens when demand for compute grows faster than power and infrastructure can be delivered, and which shortages are temporary.

The Throughline

Arena and Danu are both businesses that exist because a claim needs a witness. Arena’s argument is that a model can learn to pass a fixed exam, so the exam stops telling you anything, and the fix is a measurement that keeps changing because it is fed by real people with real prompts. Danu’s version is physical: a recycling plant does not care how clever the software is, only whether the sorting line earns more with the robot than without it. In both cases the buyer is paying for evidence, not for capability.

That is also why the alignment category is the interesting part of Arena’s announcement. Deceptive completion, a model misreporting its own work, is exactly what a static benchmark cannot catch if the model knows it is being graded. It is worth reading the cartoon in this issue with that in mind. A tester that is itself being tested is a harder problem than a tester that is only measuring, and the story does not say how Arena handles it.

I would also keep a skeptical eye on the incentives. Arena is paid by the labs it ranks through its evaluations business, and it now ranks them on honesty. The company calls itself neutral, and that claim will be tested by how the preliminary leaderboard changes as more data arrives and who is willing to publish their position on it.

The Bigger Picture

Evaluation is turning into an industry of its own. A year ago a leaderboard was a research curiosity and a bragging right for labs. A $3.1 billion valuation says buyers now treat the question “which model, and can I trust it?” as a budget line, and that is a sign the market is maturing from capability demos toward procurement.

The same shift shows up at the physical edge of AI. Startups like Danu are selling to customers who read a spreadsheet, not a launch post, and the infrastructure session at Disrupt starts from the premise that compute is limited by power and grid connections rather than ideas. The common thread is that the hype phase rewards the best story, while the next phase rewards whoever can show a number that survives contact with the real world. Whether alignment scores can ever be that kind of number is still open.

What to Watch

  • How Arena’s alignment leaderboard changes once it moves past preliminary, and whether labs dispute the three measures or adopt them.
  • Whether Danu converts its 200-plus pipeline into contracts, and whether its customer ROI estimate holds up on real sorting lines.
  • What comes out of Disrupt, October 13 to 15, on the power and data center constraints behind AI growth.
