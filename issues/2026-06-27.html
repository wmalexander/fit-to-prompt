Washington Unlocks Anthropic's Most Powerful Model, On Its Own Terms

Semafor · Jun 26

The U.S. government lifted its block on Mythos, Anthropic's most capable model, clearing it for release to more than 100 American companies and federal agencies. The decision ends a roughly two-week standoff in which Washington had restrained the model over fears about its potential for misuse, a delay that left one of the frontier's sharpest tools sitting behind a government gate while the rest of the market moved.

What makes the moment notable is not just the release but the shape of it. Access was granted to a curated list, on the administration's say-so, rather than opened to the market. The same week, the White House moved to personally vet who can use OpenAI's newest model. The throughline is unmistakable: for the most powerful systems, the binding constraint is no longer engineering or even price. It is who holds the key, and a frontier model's distribution now runs through Washington.

OpenAI Says the U.S. Government Will Vet Who Uses Its Latest Model

The Washington Post · Jun 26

OpenAI will let the U.S. government approve who gets access to its newest model, GPT-5.6, after a Trump administration request narrowed the rollout to government-vetted partners. It is an extraordinary inversion of how software usually ships: instead of a public launch with a waitlist, the customer list is filtered through Washington. OpenAI has signaled it is uneasy with the precedent even as it complies, and paired with the Mythos release it marks the week government access control became a frontier-model feature, not an afterthought.

OpenAI Limits GPT-5.6, Says Restrictions Shouldn't Be the Norm

TechCrunch · Jun 26

OpenAI confirmed it restricted its new GPT-5.6 lineup to a limited set of government-approved partners following a Trump administration request, while publicly arguing that this kind of restraint "shouldn't be the norm." The dual message, comply now, object on principle, captures the awkward position frontier labs find themselves in as national-security review starts attaching itself to ordinary product launches.

Asian Labs Rush Out Mythos-Like Models as the Export Ban Drags On

TechCrunch · Jun 27

With Anthropic's most powerful models still walled off by U.S. export rules abroad, Asian challengers are filling the gap: China's 360 unveiled Tulongfeng and Tokyo's Sakana AI launched Fugu, both pitched as alternatives to the export-banned Mythos and Fable 5. It is the predictable second-order effect of export control, restricting the frontier at home accelerates the race to reproduce it everywhere else.

✦ The Big Picture

For two years the question about a frontier model was "can it?" This week the question became "are you allowed?" The U.S. government cleared Anthropic's most powerful model, Mythos, for release, but only to a curated list of 100-plus companies and agencies, and in the same stretch moved to personally vet who can use OpenAI's GPT-5.6. The most consequential thing about the best models is no longer their capability. It is who holds the key.

▶ Listen to the Digest ~6 min

The Government Becomes the Gatekeeper

  • Mythos was released, but not to the market. Washington lifted a roughly two-week block on Anthropic's top model and authorized it for more than 100 named U.S. companies and federal agencies. Access was granted by approval, not by launch, a curated rollout that treats a commercial model like a controlled good.
  • OpenAI handed the guest list to the White House. GPT-5.6's rollout was narrowed to government-vetted partners at the administration's request. OpenAI complied while publicly arguing the restriction "shouldn't be the norm," and Zvi Mowshowitz warns the deeper shift is to opaque, ad hoc, case-by-case approval of who gets frontier access at all.
  • Export control has a predictable echo. With Mythos and Fable 5 still walled off abroad, China's 360 shipped Tulongfeng and Japan's Sakana AI shipped Fugu, both pitched as Mythos-like. Restricting the frontier at home accelerates the scramble to rebuild it everywhere else.

The Counter-Currents

  • Gary Marcus calls June the month the momentum stalled. He points to a delayed OpenAI IPO, a broad sell-off in AI-exposed stocks, and louder questions about profitability and reliability, the bear case that the binding constraint may turn out to be economics, not capability.
  • "Open" is the pressure valve everyone is watching. Doubleword's analysis finds the open-vs-closed gap closes by December 3, 2026 on one headline metric but holds steady at roughly five months across 18 benchmarks. DeepSeek, meanwhile, open-sourced DeepSpec for speculative decoding, chipping away at the cost of running models in the open.
  • Anthropic's Economic Index zooms into the clock. Drawing on hourly-level methodology and a survey of 9,700 users, it shows personal Claude use peaking on weekends and work use clustering in business hours, then links those rhythms to how people see their own jobs. AI is no longer a novelty people visit; it is woven into the working day.

His fix is an open engine with shared state, claim-locks, and receipts so agents hand off to each other, and to a human only where judgment is required.
  • Chase AI turns Claude Code into a workbench. Seventeen plugins, skills, and CLIs across design, productivity, and data, a practical map of how power users are actually assembling agentic toolchains right now.
  • Mathematicians confront the existential version. IEEE Spectrum surveys a field split between seeing AI as collaborator and fearing obsolescence, and lands on the durable point: the purpose of math was never only to generate proofs. It is the white-collar identity question every knowledge worker is now asking.

The Throughline

Strip away the individual headlines and today is a single story told from two directions. From the top, the state is asserting itself as the gatekeeper of frontier capability: Mythos released by permission, GPT-5.6 access vetted in Washington, export bans pushing rivals to build their own. From the bottom, the people actually using these systems, Nate Jones, Chase AI, Anthropic's own survey respondents, are quietly rewiring how work gets done around them. The two are connected. The moment a technology becomes powerful enough that a government wants to control who touches it is also the moment it becomes load-bearing enough to restructure daily labor.

The cartoon on today's front page is only half a joke. For two years the assumed moat in AI was the technology, the model, the training run, the talent. This week reframes the moat as access, and access is granted by the person with the clipboard. That is a profound shift in where power sits. It means the next competitive battles may be fought less in research labs and more in approval queues, and that "who is on the list" becomes a strategic question on par with "whose model is best." Anthropic and OpenAI both got their models out the door this week, but on terms set by someone else.

And the skeptics deserve a hearing precisely because the gatekeeping is so dramatic. Gary Marcus's argument, that the IPO slipped, the stocks wobbled, and the profitability questions are getting louder, sits in productive tension with the access story. If the technology were obviously a runaway commercial success, you would not expect the market to flinch. The most honest read of the week is that AI is simultaneously important enough to be nationalized in spirit and uncertain enough to spook investors. Both things are true, and the gap between them is where the next year gets decided.

The Bigger Picture

What we are watching is the early institutionalization of AI, the messy phase where a raw capability gets wrapped in rules, lists, and intermediaries. Nuclear technology, encryption, and advanced chips all went through versions of this: a period where the government decided that "who can have this" was too important to leave to the market. Frontier models are now in that bucket. The Mythos release and the GPT-5.6 vetting are not one-off political favors; they are the opening moves of a permanent access-control layer that will sit between the labs and their customers. Expect approved-user lists, export carve-outs, and discretionary gatekeeping to become as routine as SOC 2 compliance.

The second-order effects are already visible and they cut against the policy's intent. Every wall raised around the American frontier is an incentive for someone else to scale their own. Sakana's Fugu and 360's Tulongfeng are this week's examples; Doubleword's five-month gap quantifies how little runway the closed frontier really has before an open alternative is good enough. Control concentrates power in the short run and disperses it in the long run, because the knowledge of how to build these systems is not the scarce resource it was in 2023.

For everyone who is not a lab or a regulator, the practical lesson is the one the builders are already living. While the policy fight plays out above their heads, Nate Jones is killing the copy-paste hallway, Chase AI is assembling a 17-tool workbench, and Anthropic's survey respondents are folding Claude into the texture of their week. The frontier may be gated, but the diffusion of what is already out is unstoppable, and that is where most of the economic value will actually accrue. The headline is about who holds the key. The quieter, bigger story is about everyone learning to live in the house.

What to Watch

  • Whether vetting becomes the template. If government approval of frontier-model users sticks for Mythos and GPT-5.6, watch for it to harden into a standing process, with published criteria or, as Mowshowitz fears, without them.
  • How fast the open frontier closes the gap. Doubleword's December 3 prediction is a falsifiable marker. Track whether a genuinely frontier-class open model lands near that date, which would make export and access controls far less enforceable.
  • Whether Marcus's bear signal spreads. A delayed IPO and a stock wobble are not a thesis yet. Watch the next earnings and funding rounds for evidence that the profitability question is real, not seasonal.
