✦ The Big Picture

Anthropic, with a $30 billion run-rate, just locked in multi-gigawatt TPU capacity from Google and Broadcom that won't even come online until 2027, and still frames it as urgent. The same day, The New Yorker publishes a long profile asking whether Sam Altman, who is effectively steering the most influential AI company on earth, can be trusted at all. Underneath both stories is the same uncomfortable fact: the people making multi-year bets on compute and governance are asking us to trust them on faith, because nobody has the leverage to demand anything else.

Today's Headlines

Compute and the Power Question

  • Anthropic, Google, and Broadcom go all-in on TPUs. Anthropic confirmed a multi-year expansion that secures multiple gigawatts of next-generation TPU capacity starting in 2027. The company pinned the deal to Claude's frontier roadmap and "extraordinary" demand, disclosing run-rate revenue above $30 billion. Google is simultaneously Anthropic's investor, supplier, and cloud rival, a three-way entanglement that would have been unthinkable two years ago.

Governance and Trust

  • "Sam Altman May Control Our Future. Can He Be Trusted?" Ronan Farrow and Andrew Marantz revisit the persistent credibility questions around OpenAI's CEO, drawing on new interviews and internal documents. The piece lands at a moment when OpenAI is simultaneously pushing safety-fellowship programs and publishing its own policy recommendations, making Altman's personal credibility a proxy for the whole company's.
  • OpenAI Safety Fellowship. OpenAI announced a new external-researcher fellowship aimed at expanding its safety and alignment bench, explicitly pitched as deepening collaboration with outside experts.
  • OpenAI's "AI Progress and Recommendations." A companion post lays out OpenAI's view of where capabilities are heading and the policy asks it would like governments and industry to adopt. Published alongside the fellowship, the two releases read as a coordinated effort to reshape the conversation right as the New Yorker profile drops.

Claude Code Keeps Shipping

  • Vim visual modes, unified /usage, custom themes. Recent Claude Code releases (through 2.1.118) added vim visual modes, merged /cost and /stats into a single /usage command, introduced user-defined themes, let MCP tools be invoked from hooks, and closed several OAuth and MCP auth edge cases. Earlier entries landed a 67% speedup on /resume for large sessions, smoother fullscreen scrolling, thinking-progress indicators, and improved plugin dependency resolution.
  • "Claude Just Changed Overnight" (Wes Roth). Roth frames the latest round of Claude changes as a step-change rather than a patch, connecting the model-side updates to Anthropic's compute expansion and arguing that Claude's positioning against GPT and Gemini is shifting faster than most users have noticed.
  • "Did Claude Code Just Get Plan Mode 2.0?" (Chase AI). A hands-on walkthrough of how the planner now structures multi-step work and surfaces intent before execution, obsoleting several community workarounds that developers had wired up around the older plan behavior.

Media and the Messy Middle

  • WSJ holds up Fortune as the newsroom-AI model. Editor-in-Chief Emma Tucker publicly commended Fortune's transparent approach to deploying AI-generated content and told her own staff to study it. This is a legacy-media EIC explicitly endorsing another publication's AI playbook, a rare move that only makes sense if newsrooms have concluded the "wait and see" posture is no longer tenable.

The pitch is that agents need real compute, not just tool-call shims.

The Throughline

Today's stories sit along a single axis: who gets to decide, and on what evidence. Anthropic is asking customers, regulators, and the public to trust that locking in multi-gigawatt TPU capacity through 2027 (with Google and Broadcom as both partner and rival) is the right use of capital at a $30B run-rate. OpenAI is asking the same audience to trust that a safety fellowship and a policy document add up to meaningful governance, right as The New Yorker publishes a piece whose headline question is "can he be trusted?" These are not unrelated asks. They are the same ask, laundered through different institutional forms.

The developer-tools layer tells the same story in miniature. Claude Code keeps shipping, vim visual modes, a unified /usage command, custom themes, faster /resume, and Wes Roth's "Claude just changed overnight" framing captures something real: the tool is evolving fast enough that yesterday's muscle memory is already stale. Chase AI's plan-mode walkthrough is downstream of the same phenomenon. Community workarounds that existed for months are now obsolete because the upstream shipped past them. The pattern rewards teams plugged into the Discord and punishes anyone who tried to build durable abstractions.

The infrastructure releases, Freestyle and Hippo-Memory, are the practical response. If agents are going to produce at a different cadence than organizations can absorb, you need substrates that treat agents as first-class citizens: real VMs with sub-600ms cold starts, memory systems that model decay and consolidation, storage that survives a session. These are not cosmetic libraries. They are an acknowledgement that the last two years of "just wrap it in a prompt" infrastructure has hit its limits.

What ties it all together is the asymmetry between the speed of deployment and the speed of scrutiny. Anthropic is booking 2027 compute today. Claude Code is shipping user-visible changes fast enough to reset habits. Fortune is deploying AI-generated content in a legacy newsroom with the WSJ's EIC holding it up as the template. Each of these is a bet that the window for acting faster than the oversight layer is still open, and that the payoff from acting now outweighs the cost of whatever scrutiny lands later.

The Bigger Picture

Zoom out and today's stories look like an inflection. The compute-capacity conversation has moved from "can we get GPUs this year?" to "which five-year TPU allocation do we sign in 2027?" That is a qualitative shift. Infrastructure commitments of this size demand revenue stories that extend far past today's enterprise adoption curves. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google are each implicitly forecasting a world where AI usage grows so dramatically that the binding constraint is silicon, not software. The Broadcom deal is both evidence for that forecast and a self-fulfilling mechanism that makes it more likely to come true.

Against that capital backdrop, the governance conversation is running on a different timescale. The New Yorker piece, OpenAI's safety fellowship, and OpenAI's own progress-and-recommendations post are all trying to answer the same question: if trust in individual founders is the weak link, what institutional forms can carry the weight instead? Today's answer is unsatisfying, fellowships, position papers, and long-form journalism, none of which have the leverage to change a multi-year TPU commitment. The gap between what the capital layer is committing to and what the governance layer can scrutinize has never been wider.

The developer-tools and agent-infrastructure stories, Claude Code's cadence, Freestyle's VMs, Hippo-Memory's consolidation model, are the ground-level evidence that the industry is quietly rewiring itself around the assumption that AI agents will be doing a meaningful share of real work in production by the time those 2027 TPUs come online. The question is not whether the substrate gets built. It's whether the institutions around it catch up in time to have any say in how it's used.

What to Watch

  • Whether Google's dual role as Anthropic partner and competitor draws antitrust attention. A multi-gigawatt TPU supply agreement with a major investor and product rival is exactly the kind of structure that gets flagged once compute becomes publicly visible as a strategic asset, not just a line item.
  • How OpenAI's governance output is received. The safety fellowship and the recommendations post are the most concrete institutional moves OpenAI has made in months. If independent researchers and policy shops respond skeptically, expect the Altman-credibility conversation from The New Yorker to become the default frame.
  • Agent infrastructure adoption curves. Freestyle and Hippo-Memory are bets that the next layer of AI tooling looks less like "wrap an API" and more like "give the agent its own machine and its own memory." Watch the star counts and production case studies over the next 90 days.
