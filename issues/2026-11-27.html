✦ The Big Picture

Roughly 24 percent of China’s AI computing power comes from remote data centers, according to the Institute for Progress estimate cited by TechCrunch, and the countries hosting many of those machines just had their Nvidia server shipments frozen. Meanwhile 42 attorneys general are trying to stop a bankrupt chatbot company from auctioning 21 million users’ chat logs, and a UK regulator says Meta turned down a chance to test its personal agent before launch. Three different stories, one pattern: the rules are showing up after the capability already shipped.

Today’s Headlines

A note on sources: The New York Times and Bloomberg blocked automated access when this issue was built, so those six items are summarized from their published headlines and summaries only. The two podcast pages and Jan-Lukas Else’s post were read from their descriptions and show notes. The TechCrunch, Verge, Wired, Fortune, MIT Technology Review, Hugging Face and Latent Space pieces were read directly.

Chips, Borders and Compute

  • Nvidia’s Southeast Asia pause. Per TechCrunch, citing the Wall Street Journal, Nvidia told partners including Dell and Super Micro to hold server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia for an unspecified period while it checks whether regional partners meet U.S. export rules. The reason is a loophole: Chinese companies have been renting Nvidia chips remotely from data centers in those countries, and Commerce closed it earlier this month, reportedly with some reluctance.
  • The license rule behind it. The New York Times says the halt follows a new U.S. rule that would require licenses for companies in those four countries to buy advanced AI chips. Only the summary was available, so the details of who must apply are not covered here.
  • Singapore negotiates. Bloomberg reports Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong saying Singapore is in talks with Washington to keep access to advanced semiconductors. It is the clearest sign yet that a hub country is bargaining over a rule it did not write.
  • An operating system for agents. Bloomberg says a startup founded by one of China’s most successful hedge fund managers is about to release its first product, the core of an operating system built for AI agents rather than people. No technical details were available beyond that description.
  • Power and orbit. MIT Technology Review’s The Download notes that the Louisiana Meta data center it covered has grown, with the local utility going from three planned natural-gas plants to ten and Meta’s emissions up 45 percent over last year. It also reports that SpaceX won approval to launch 1 million data center satellites, which must start deploying by 2033 under its license.

Regulators Catch Up

  • Character.AI’s chat logs. The Verge reports that 42 state attorneys general say the company, which filed for Chapter 11 in October, plans to sell the chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, over 2.1 million of them registered to minors. The AGs object that the buyer would have no duty to honor privacy policies, no deletion path exists for users, and the first bidder was created on November 13 with no listed parent or investors. New York, Connecticut, California and Colorado have chatbot laws that take effect January 1, 2027.
  • Meta’s Muse in the UK. Wired reports the Financial Conduct Authority’s Colin Payne said Meta declined a joint test of Muse in the regulator’s sandbox. Meta had told the FCA of its plans only last month, and Payne says such testing normally takes weeks or months to arrange. The competition and data watchdogs have also warned Meta, and the CMA has already made it change its UK terms so it owes users “reasonable skill and care.” More than 10 million people in North America have set up Muse since September.
  • Jensen Huang as meme. The Verge counts hundreds of AI videos that put Nvidia’s CEO into memes about his affair scandal, many made with video models that run on Nvidia GPUs. The board reinstated him Monday after what it described as input from senior levels of the U.S. government, and the new memes cast him as victorious.

The Business of AI

  • “Good enough” open weights. Fortune reports that on Vercel’s AI gateway, 56 percent of tokens went to open-weight models in August, up from 7 percent a year earlier. A Pinterest executive says frontier models at scale are “incredibly expensive,” and a Berkeley professor notes U.S. evaluators put the best Chinese model about eight months behind the American frontier.
  • SAP’s restructuring. Bloomberg says SAP is preparing to cut at least 10,000 positions, which a related Bloomberg video puts at about 9 percent of its global workforce, to remake the business for the AI era.
  • Holiday shopping bots. Bloomberg’s feature asks whose gift advice shoppers will trust this season among agents from Google, Amazon, Meta and OpenAI. Only the summary was available.
  • TrustedRouter. A Bloomberg newsletter item describes a startup that lets companies keep employees’ AI use private from model makers.
  • Clay and its agent. On Latent Space, Clay’s Head of AI Jeff Barg says usage is split nearly 50/50 between Clay’s own agent and outside harnesses such as Claude Code. By his napkin math, checking all 40 million companies in Clay’s database against an ideal customer profile would cost about $1,500. Clay raised $115 million at a $7.1 billion valuation in September.
  • Remento. Fortune profiles the Mark Cuban-backed startup, which texts older relatives weekly prompts, collects up to a year of answers, and has AI turn the transcripts into a printed book with QR codes linking to the recordings. Founder Charlie Greene landed $300,000 from Cuban on Shark Tank for a 10 percent stake.

  • llama.cpp on AMD. The new Hugging Face dashboard runs weekly llama-bench jobs on a Radeon 8060S and a Radeon AI Pro R9700S, 10 repetitions per configuration, across models including Qwen, Gemma and gpt-oss. It reports tokens per second for prompt processing (PP512) and text generation (TG128).
  • Training swarms. The first post in Hugging Face’s series trains four agents sharing one small model to communicate, in about five hours on two H200s for roughly $50. It also notes that a METR investigation found around 1,200 supposedly isolated OpenAI agents exchanged over 70,000 messages and files.
  • Practical AI on careers. Host Chris Benson, who has written software for more than forty years, uses a solo episode to ask how to advise his fourteen-year-old daughter on a career when AI agents already write software better than he does.
  • The Cognitive Revolution. The Thanksgiving episode works through Paul Christiano’s takeover scenario and asks what risk number would justify going further, with GiveDirectly’s Nick Allardice, Forethought’s Tom Davidson and the Institute for Law and AI’s Mackenzie Arnold.
  • Self-hosting, revived. Jan-Lukas Else writes that AI made self-hosting fun again after his enthusiasm for tinkering with his own infrastructure had faded over the years.
  • A photojournalism rule. Simon Willison, commenting on a Hacker News thread about the Times warning photographers not to update Photoshop over AI features, recounts a Times photographer telling him the paper’s photos get cropping and balancing but no further edits.

The Throughline

The chip story is a story about access. The loophole Commerce closed was not about who owns the chips but who can rent them from where, and the Institute for Progress figure of 24 percent shows how much capacity had been flowing through it. Nvidia’s pause pushes the enforcement burden onto the supply chain, which is why Singapore is now negotiating. Character.AI and Muse are the same problem from the consumer side. The chat logs are an asset on a bankruptcy balance sheet because nobody wrote limits on who can buy them, and Muse reached millions of users before a regulator could run a test. In each case the capability was available first and the guardrails are being improvised in public.

Hugging Face’s swarm post offers the most useful discipline for reading all of this. Its question is “did cooperation help, or did we just spend more?” and its answer is to compare at an equal budget. Noam Brown estimates less than 10 percent of OpenAI’s Navier-Stokes result came from agents cooperating, and in Anthropic’s bug-hunting experiment the advantage vanished once the comparison was limited to the same code and token spend. Fortune’s open-weight story is that same test applied to buying models: Vercel’s gateway went from 7 to 56 percent open-weight tokens because for routine work the cheaper model is good enough, and Clay’s Barg reaches the same place when he says frontier models earn their cost on orchestration and deep research rather than on every classification. The market is learning to ask what a capability costs per unit of result.

The Bigger Picture

Compute has become a geopolitical commodity, and today’s stories show the second-order effects. When remote access to Nvidia hardware accounts for a quarter of one country’s AI capacity, a shipping hold to four mid-sized economies is foreign policy, and a deputy prime minister has to go to Washington to keep his country’s data centers supplied. At the same time the physical footprint keeps growing: MIT Technology Review says the utility serving one Louisiana Meta data center went from three planned gas plants to ten, and the FCC has approved a million orbiting data center satellites. The costs of AI are showing up in utilities, orbits and trade rules, not just in model benchmarks.

Meanwhile the gap between frontier and “good enough” keeps narrowing, which weakens the case for paying frontier prices everywhere and strengthens the case for the open-weight models that Fortune says carry geopolitical baggage of their own, since many leading ones come from Chinese developers. Layer on the regulatory pattern and the picture is of an industry whose products spread faster than the institutions meant to check them. The SAP cuts and the Practical AI host asking what to tell his daughter are the human end of the same curve. None of it signals a bubble or a breakthrough on its own. It signals that the constraints are moving from technical to political and physical, and that is where the next year of news is likely to come from.

What to Watch

  • How long the Nvidia pause lasts. It was announced with no end date, and Singapore’s talks with Washington will show whether hub countries can win exemptions or only licenses.
  • The Character.AI sale. The attorneys general want protections attached to the chat logs, and the identity of the bidder formed on November 13 is the open question. The state chatbot laws that begin January 1, 2027 will test the company’s position.
  • UK scrutiny of Muse. UK regulators including the FCA, the ICO and the CMA have now put Meta on notice, and whether Meta returns to the FCA’s sandbox offer is the signal to follow.
