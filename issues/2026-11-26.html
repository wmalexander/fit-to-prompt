✦ The Big Picture

Twenty terabytes of data come off an airplane engine in a single hour of flying, according to the International Air Transport Association, and a few researchers now think the planes’ ordinary position broadcasts are enough to spot turbulence nobody can see. Google, meanwhile, filed a patent for a system that would use what it knows about you to generate an endless feed of AI videos in which you play the hero. One story is about using data to make a hard physical problem more predictable. The other is about using data to make entertainment more personal, and the difference in ambition says a lot about where AI effort is going.

Today’s Headlines

A note on sources: Bloomberg blocked automated access when this issue was built, so its seven items are summarized from published headlines only, and the nine YouTube Shorts are listed by title. The Verge, Wired and Hugging Face pieces were read in full.

AI Gets Personal and Physical

  • Google’s “My Channel.” The patent was filed last May and published last week. It describes building prompts from a person’s demographics and viewing history, plus device data such as IP address, GPS location and age, and from current news like sports scores. An LLM would turn those prompts into videos and ads generated on Google’s servers, up to entire movies, and the system could link to other streaming services for more data. Its most unsettling feature, in The Verge’s telling, is the use of photos, video and audio of a user to create an avatar that becomes “the protagonist or lead character.” The patent has been rejected, but Google could appeal or resubmit.
  • Turbulence, with a data assist. Wired reports that more than 200 people were seriously injured on US airplanes in turbulence incidents between 2009 and 2023, per FAA data, and that climate change is making sudden clear-air turbulence worse. NCAR physicist Larry Cornman and an FAA air traffic controller found that the position, altitude and speed broadcasts aircraft already must send can reveal unexpected turbulence without new onboard hardware. In September the National Science Foundation announced a $30 million award to nine universities for a center called Tempest, which will study turbulence in air, fusion reactors and gas clouds in space.

Chips, Robots and Money

  • SoftBank’s credit risk. Bloomberg’s headline says the cost of insuring SoftBank’s debt topped 500 basis points as AI worries mount. The article itself was not accessible, so the drivers behind the move are not summarized here.
  • Xpeng’s humanoid. Bloomberg reports Xpeng says its humanoid robot will cost the same as an EV.
  • Huawei’s memory chip. Huawei teased a next-generation AI memory chip aimed at Samsung and SK Hynix, per Bloomberg. A related Bloomberg video says US chip curbs have stalled data center financing in Asia.
  • On the wire. Musk says every US child will get two SpaceX shares, doubling an earlier pledge. Microdramas are reported to have earned $20 billion, and Apple plans a new store in Hyderabad.

Open Models and Research

  • NVIDIA opens its parsing data. Alongside Nemotron 3.5 Parse, NVIDIA released Synthetic Charts V1 (9.98M examples), Synthetic Tables V1 (1M) and OCR Synthetic Multilingual v2 (7.9M), plus a fine-tuning recipe. In a controlled comparison, adding reinforcement learning after supervised fine-tuning cut looping outputs from 126 pages to 29, about 77% fewer, and raised the overall score from 66.24 to 71.38, with gains of 5.18 points on tables and 4.15 on charts. A companion speculator drafts up to 32 tokens ahead and averages about 14 accepted per step.
  • Hard Fork, Ezra Klein and creators. The Shorts in The Wire include Hard Fork clips on Jensen Huang and Grand Theft Auto VI, an Ezra Klein Show clip on the low-trust internet, and Claude Code testing tips from Leon van Zyl.

The Throughline

The Wired and Verge stories are both about what happens when AI meets a flood of data, and they land in opposite places. Aviation has more data than it can use: 20 terabytes an hour per engine, plus public broadcasts, plus satellites. The hard part is turning it into a forecast, and the researchers are honest that it will never be perfect, because tiny changes in wind or temperature can alter the flow. Google’s patent starts from the same raw material, data about people, and uses it to maximize attention rather than to reduce harm.

NVIDIA’s Parse release is a quieter version of the same lesson. Its chart gains came from synthetic data with known answers, rendering variations and training the model to reverse them. That is the pattern behind most of today’s progress: a verifiable target, a reward that checks it, and a lot of generated examples. Turbulence prediction and document parsing are both problems where you can check the answer, which is why they improve. A personalized entertainment feed has no such check, only engagement.

The Bigger Picture

Wired quotes Michael Murillo of Michigan State saying turbulence “has been a problem only human minds could work on; as of this year, that’s no longer true,” pointing to OpenAI’s fall announcement that it had resolved the Navier-Stokes problem. Whether or not that claim holds up, it signals where scientists now expect help from AI: hard physics, with fusion energy tied to the same turbulence problem.

The consumer side looks different. A rejected patent is not a product, and The Verge itself says it may never ship. Still, the filing shows how companies think about the next feed: not recommending content made by others, but generating content made for you. The credit-market worry about SoftBank in the same day’s headlines is a reminder that the money behind these bets is being watched more closely.

What to Watch

  • Google’s next patent move. The Verge says Google could appeal or resubmit an amended version of My Channel.
  • The Tempest center. The $30 million NSF award is new, so early results on turbulence prediction are the thing to look for.
  • SoftBank’s credit spread. It crossed 500 basis points today, and whether it keeps widening will show how nervous lenders are about AI exposure.
