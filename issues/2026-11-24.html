✦ The Big Picture

Nvidia shares rose more than 30% on Monday to close at $165.05, and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq posted their best day since April 2025, up 4.7% and 6.5%. The trigger was a personnel decision: a board that had said days earlier it “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO” brought him back, and said it did so after “input from senior levels of the United States government.” When one reversal moves the whole market, the market is telling you what it thinks the AI trade is made of.

Today’s Headlines

A note on sources: Bloomberg, Reuters and The Washington Post blocked automated access when this issue was built, so their items are summarized from published headlines only. Three further queued stories (one AP and two Reuters items) were left out because their headlines could not be confirmed. The two Fortune pieces are drawn from the articles themselves, and the details below come from them.

Huang Returns

  • Eleven days, start to finish. Fortune’s reconstruction runs from an Oct. 29 New York Times report that U.S. intelligence officials believed Megaspeed executive Alice Huang (no relation) was passing information to the Chinese government, through Huang’s admission on Nov. 8 of an affair, to his resignation, with CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO and Harvey Jones as Nvidia’s first board chair. Shares fell as much as 14.35% at the low, erasing about $600 billion, and closed down 4.34%.
  • A comeback campaign. An Nvidia employee’s open letter demanding reinstatement had more than 206,000 names by Monday, including Elon Musk, Tobias Lutke and Tim Sweeney. Trump posted on Sunday calling for the rehire, and Fortune cites Semafor reporting that he dialed into the board meeting that made the decision. “I believe my intervention was very important,” Trump told CNBC.
  • Why the board flipped. Fortune’s second piece weighs three explanations: the selloff, the president, or recession fear. Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon called the board’s on-again, off-again response “clown-like” yet upgraded the stock to outperform, while Gabelli’s Ryuta Makino said it proves the board has “severely lacking corporate governance.”
  • The same story, from other desks. The Washington Post’s headline says the board reinstated Huang “after US government input,” matching the board’s own statement, and its business and opinion pages carry “He’s So Back” and “leaves AI questions unresolved.” Bloomberg has video on “Trump’s role in Huang’s return” and “the path ahead,” and Reuters gathered analyst comment on the market impact.

Markets and the AI Trade

  • The rebound reaches Asia. Bloomberg’s headline says Japan stocks rebounded as Huang’s return “resuscitates” the AI trade. Fortune puts the scale in context: last week the S&P 500 fell 8.6%, its worst week since April 2025, and the Nasdaq about 10%, its worst since March 2020. Monday recovered roughly half the S&P’s loss and more than half the Nasdaq’s.
  • A reckoning on returns. The Bloomberg video headline has Rajan warning the AI boom faces a reckoning on returns, a caution that sits awkwardly beside a one-day rally built on a CEO announcement.
  • The boom still shows up in the numbers. Bloomberg says Singapore raised its GDP growth outlook to around 6% on the AI boom, and a Fujitsu CTO interview covers the company’s AI focus and outlook. Fortune adds that AI investment added roughly 1.3 percentage points to U.S. real GDP growth over the past three quarters, per Reuters citing Apollo data.

Blacklists and Probes

  • Megaspeed is cut off. Fortune reports the Commerce Department added Megaspeed to a blacklist on Monday, barring it from receiving Nvidia chips without a license, even as Huang returned. The Post’s headline calls it a key firm in the Nvidia scandal, and Reuters reports Singapore is investigating the Nvidia-linked company after the blacklisting.
  • The compliance burden spreads. Bloomberg’s headlines say Malaysia told data centers to cut off firms on the US blacklist, and that the US blacklisted an Alibaba-owned company in a setback to its AI ambitions. The first suggests the list is now enforced by the hosts, not just the exporters.
  • Investigations remain open. Fortune lists an internal investigation run by Wachtell, Lipton, a reported SEC probe into whether Nvidia’s disclosures misled investors, a subpoena from California Attorney General Rob Bonta, and a congressional inquiry into approval of H200 chip sales to China. Reuters has an explainer on the various federal and state probes.

The Throughline

Fortune says outright that investors were “treating Huang as a stand-in for the AI trade,” and Monday proved the point in reverse. Nvidia reported revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103%, the same day its board said Huang was not coming back, and the stock still could not catch a break. Governance experts quoted by Fortune read the reinstatement as an attempt to restore market confidence, which is a different thing from restoring the confidence itself.

The unresolved parts are the ones that moved no stock price on Monday. Fortune notes the investigation of Huang as a possible target of foreign intelligence efforts is not finished, Megaspeed was blacklisted the same day, and the older worries about Nvidia acting as a “central bank” financing startups and possibly masking true demand “have not gone away.” If anything, Fortune says, his return makes that practice less likely to end, since the interim CEO had signaled willingness to reassess it.

The politics are the most uncomfortable thread. Trump said “I made the deal,” the White House said he was “totally involved,” and Fortune adds that CNBC found accounts tied to the president made 103 trades since his return to office, worth a cumulative $27 million to $97.5 million, which the White House says outside advisors control. None of that proves a conflict, but a market that rallies 30% on a phone call from the president has a transparency problem however the call went.

The Bigger Picture

A year ago a chip company’s leadership dispute would have been a business story. Now Nvidia’s market value is part of the macro picture: if AI spending adds more than a point to GDP growth, as the Apollo figure Fortune cites suggests, then a CEO exit that costs $1 trillion in market value is read in Washington as a threat to growth and to the U.S. lead over China. That is why the intervention came from the top, and why it will be hard to describe the next Nvidia crisis as a purely private matter.

The blacklist headlines point the other way. The U.S. is tightening who can buy Nvidia chips at the same moment it is spending political capital to keep the company’s leader. Malaysia and Singapore, the hosts for much of this compute, are now being drawn into enforcement. The pattern to watch is less about any one executive than about AI infrastructure becoming a place where finance, geopolitics and personal loyalty are the same story.

What to Watch

  • The investigations. The Wachtell review, the SEC inquiry and California’s subpoena can each change the story, and none has reported yet.
  • Southeast Asia as the enforcement layer. Watch whether Malaysia’s instruction to data centers and Singapore’s Megaspeed probe become a template for other hosting countries.
  • Whether Monday holds. Fortune says Nvidia shed roughly $1 trillion after the resignation and regained part of it in one session. Follow-through, not the first day, shows whether the market bought the reversal or the relief.
